The acceleration of an object is the net force on it divided by its mass: the same push speeds a light object up a lot and a heavy one only a little.

## The idea
> Push a shopping trolley, then push a car. Same push, very different result.

<div class="read">
<p>Push an empty shopping trolley and it shoots forward. Push a full one just as hard and it creeps. Push a parked car and, as far as you can tell, nothing happens at all. In each case your push was the same. What changed was how much stuff you were trying to move.</p>
<p>Now try it the other way round. Push the empty trolley gently, then push it as hard as you can. The harder push makes it speed up faster. So two things decide how quickly something changes its motion: how hard you push it, and how much of it there is.</p>
<p>The first law said that a force is needed to change motion. The second law says <strong>how much</strong> the motion changes. It turns "a push makes things speed up" into a rule with numbers, and that rule is what lets engineers work out how big a rocket engine must be, how long a car needs to stop, or how hard a footballer has to kick.</p>
<div class="aside"><h4>Try it at home</h4><p>Roll an empty plastic bottle and a full one across the floor with the same flick of your finger. Watch which one speeds up more, and which one is harder to stop.</p></div>
</div>

## The law
> One sentence in words, one line in maths. Both say the same thing.

<div class="read">
<p>Newton's second law, in the English translation of the <em>Principia</em> published in 1729, reads:</p>
</div>
<div class="pull"><blockquote>The alteration of motion is ever proportional to the motive force impressed; and is made in the direction of the right line in which that force is impressed.</blockquote><cite>Isaac Newton, Principia, 1687</cite></div>
<div class="read">
<p>In today's words: the <dfn title="How quickly the velocity changes, in metres per second, every second">acceleration</dfn> of an object is equal to the net force on it divided by its mass, and it points the same way as the net force. Push twice as hard and the acceleration doubles. Make the object twice as heavy and the acceleration halves.</p>
<p>"Acceleration" in physics means any change of velocity: speeding up, slowing down or turning. Braking is an acceleration backwards. Going round a bend at a steady speed is an acceleration sideways. The second law covers all of them with one rule.</p>
</div>
<div class="wide">
<div class="formula">
<div class="big">
<div class="math">ΣF = m · a <i>⟺</i> a = ΣF / m</div>
<p>The net force on an object equals its mass times its acceleration. Read the other way: the acceleration is the net force shared out over the mass.</p>
</div>
<div class="keys">
<h4>What the symbols mean</h4>
<dl>
<dt>ΣF</dt><dd><b>Net force</b>All the forces on the object added together, in newtons (N).</dd>
<dt>m</dt><dd><b>Mass</b>How much matter the object has, in kilograms (kg). It is also how hard the object is to speed up or slow down.</dd>
<dt>a</dt><dd><b>Acceleration</b>How quickly the velocity changes, in metres per second squared (m/s²).</dd>
<dt>⟺</dt><dd><b>"Says the same as"</b>Both forms are the same law, rearranged.</dd>
</dl>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="read">
<p>The law also defines the unit of force. One newton is the force that gives a mass of one kilogram an acceleration of one metre per second squared. An apple in your hand presses down with about one newton.</p>
<p>The first law is hidden inside the second. If the net force is zero, the acceleration is zero, and the velocity does not change. That is exactly what the first law says.</p>
<details class="deeper"><summary>Go deeper: momentum, Newton's own version</summary><div class="in">
<p>Newton did not write "F = ma". He talked about the "alteration of motion", and by motion he meant what we now call <strong>momentum</strong>: mass times velocity, p = m · v. His law says that the force equals how fast the momentum changes.</p>
<p>For an object whose mass stays the same, that is the same as F = m · a. But the momentum form is more general. A rocket burns fuel and gets lighter every second, so its mass is not constant, and engineers use the momentum form of the law to work out how it speeds up.</p>
</div></details>
<details class="deeper"><summary>Go deeper: what if the force does not change?</summary><div class="in">
<p>When the net force stays the same, the acceleration stays the same too, and the motion can be worked out exactly. Starting with velocity v₀, after a time t the velocity is v = v₀ + a · t, and the distance covered is s = v₀ · t + ½ · a · t².</p>
<p>The ½ · a · t² part is why a car that speeds up steadily covers four times the distance in twice the time. The simulation at the end of this topic uses this: the gap between two boxes pushed with different accelerations grows with the square of the time.</p>
</div></details>
</div>

## Worked examples
> The law in action, step by step, with real numbers.

<div class="read">
<h3 style="margin-top:0">One thruster, two spacecraft</h3>
<p>A small thruster pushes with a force of 300 N. It is fixed first to a spacecraft of 1,000 kg, then to a probe of 100 kg. Out in space, with no other forces, how fast does each one speed up?</p>
<p>The net force is just the thruster: 300 N. For the spacecraft, a = 300 / 1,000 = 0.3 m/s². Every second, it goes 0.3 m/s faster. For the probe, a = 300 / 100 = 3 m/s², ten times more. Same engine, ten times lighter, ten times the acceleration.</p>
<h3>A car that brakes</h3>
<p>A car of 1,200 kg is moving at 20 m/s, about 72 km/h. Its brakes can produce a backward force of 9,600 N. How long does it take to stop?</p>
<p>The acceleration is a = 9,600 / 1,200 = 8 m/s², backwards. Each second the car loses 8 m/s of speed, so it needs 20 / 8 = 2.5 seconds to stop. In that time it travels on average at 10 m/s, so it covers about 25 metres. A car twice as heavy, with the same brakes, would need twice the time and twice the distance.</p>
<h3>Two forces at once</h3>
<p>A box of 5 kg is pushed to the right with 30 N, while friction pulls it to the left with 10 N. What is its acceleration?</p>
<p>First find the net force: 30 N to the right minus 10 N to the left leaves 20 N to the right. Then a = 20 / 5 = 4 m/s², to the right. The law always uses the net force. Forgetting the friction would give 6 m/s², which is wrong.</p>
</div>

## In everyday life
> Every time you speed up, slow down or turn, the second law is doing the maths.

<div class="read">
<p>A football is light, about 0.45 kg. A kick lasts only a hundredth of a second, but during that time the foot pushes with a force of more than a thousand newtons, and the ball leaves at around 30 m/s. Kick a bowling ball, about fifteen times heavier, just as hard, and it barely rolls, and your foot hurts. The ball's mass is what pushes back against the change.</p>
<p>Lorries need much longer to stop than cars. Their brakes are bigger, but they are also carrying ten or twenty times the mass. The second law says that for the same braking force, more mass means less deceleration, and a longer stopping distance. That is why lorries keep more distance on motorways.</p>
<p>Sports cars are built light and given powerful engines for the same reason in reverse: a large force on a small mass gives a large acceleration. Racing bicycles, running shoes and aircraft are all designed by people who think about the second law every day.</p>
<p>Even standing in a lift is the second law. When the lift starts going up, the floor has to push you up harder than your weight to speed you up, and you feel heavier for a moment. When it slows down at the top, the floor pushes less, and you feel lighter.</p>
</div>

## In space
> From a small thruster to a rocket leaving the ground.

<div class="read">
<p>To get a rocket off the ground, the engines must push harder than the rocket's weight. Whatever force is left over, the net force, goes into acceleration. The Saturn V that carried Apollo 11 weighed almost 3,000 tonnes at launch, so gravity pulled it down with about 29 million newtons. Its five engines pushed up with about 34 million newtons. The difference, about 5 million newtons, gave it an acceleration at lift-off of a little under 2 m/s², slow enough that it seemed to hang in the air for a moment.</p>
<p>Then something interesting happens. The engines burn tonnes of fuel every second, so the rocket gets lighter while the push stays about the same. With a smaller mass, the same force gives more acceleration. That is why astronauts are pressed harder and harder into their seats as a stage burns, until the engine shuts down.</p>
<p>Spacecraft far from Earth often use tiny engines. An ion engine pushes with less than a newton, about the weight of a sheet of paper. On a spacecraft of a tonne, that gives an acceleration you would never feel. But the engine can run for months without stopping, and the second law adds up every second of it into a huge change in speed.</p>
</div>

## How we found out
> From Galileo's falling balls to Newton's law of force.

<div class="read">
<div class="timeline-h">
<div><b>About 1600</b><h4>Galileo</h4><p>Shows that falling objects speed up steadily, gaining the same speed every second.</p></div>
<div><b>1673</b><h4>Huygens</h4><p>Works out the force needed to keep something moving in a circle.</p></div>
<div><b>1687</b><h4>Newton</h4><p>Publishes the second law: the change of motion is proportional to the force.</p></div>
<div><b>1750</b><h4>Euler</h4><p>Writes the law in the form F = m · a that is used today.</p></div>
<div><b>1889</b><h4>The kilogram</h4><p>A metal cylinder in Paris becomes the world standard of mass.</p></div>
<div><b>1948</b><h4>The newton</h4><p>The unit of force is named after Newton.</p></div>
</div>
<p>Galileo never wrote down a law of force, but he measured what forces do. Rolling balls down gentle slopes, he found that their speed grows by the same amount in each equal stretch of time. Something steady, gravity, was causing a steady change of motion.</p>
<p>Newton made this general. In the <em>Principia</em> he said that any force, not just gravity, changes motion in proportion to its size and in its own direction. With that one rule, together with his law of gravity, he could calculate the paths of planets, moons and comets. The same law that describes a ball rolling down a slope describes the Moon going around the Earth.</p>
<p>The short form F = m · a came later, from the Swiss mathematician Leonhard Euler, who wrote Newton's ideas in the kind of equations physicists still use.</p>
</div>

## Common mistakes
> Three ideas that sound right and are not.

<div class="read">
<div class="myth"><div class="no"><span>Not true</span><p>A bigger force means a bigger speed.</p></div><div class="yes"><span>What really happens</span><p>A bigger force means a bigger change of speed each second. A small force acting for a long time can produce a huge speed.</p></div></div>
<div class="myth"><div class="no"><span>Not true</span><p>Heavy objects need a force to keep them moving, light ones do not.</p></div><div class="yes"><span>What really happens</span><p>Neither needs a force to keep moving. Mass only decides how hard it is to change their motion.</p></div></div>
<div class="myth"><div class="no"><span>Not true</span><p>Mass and weight are the same thing.</p></div><div class="yes"><span>What really happens</span><p>Mass is how much matter there is, in kilograms. Weight is the force of gravity on it, in newtons. On the Moon your weight is a sixth of what it is on Earth, but your mass is the same.</p></div></div>
</div>
