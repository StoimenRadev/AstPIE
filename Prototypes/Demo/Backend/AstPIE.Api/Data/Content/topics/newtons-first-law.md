An object keeps moving at the same speed in the same direction, or stays at rest, until a force changes that.

## The idea
> Everything you push eventually stops. So it seems obvious that moving needs a push. It is wrong.

<div class="read">
<p>Give a book a shove across a table and it slides for a moment, then stops. Kick a football across grass and it rolls, slows and comes to rest. Stop pedalling a bicycle and sooner or later you are standing still. Every single thing you have ever seen moving has, in the end, stopped. It is easy to conclude that this is simply what motion does: it runs out, the way a battery runs out, unless something keeps topping it up.</p>
<p>Now try the same shove in different places. On a carpet the book stops almost at once. On a smooth wooden table it goes further. On a sheet of ice, or on an air hockey table where a cushion of air lifts the puck, it glides on and on, and seems almost reluctant to stop at all. Nothing about the push changed. What changed was the surface underneath.</p>
<div class="aside"><h4>Try it at home</h4><p>Slide a coin across a rug, then across a smooth floor, with the same flick. Measure how far it goes each time.</p></div>
<p>That is the clue. The book does not stop because its motion runs out. It stops because something is <em>taking</em> its motion away. On the carpet, the fibres grab at it. On the table, the tiny bumps of the two surfaces catch on each other. We call this <dfn title="A force that acts against sliding, wherever two surfaces rub together">friction</dfn>. Air does the same thing more gently: it pushes against anything moving through it. The smoother the surface and the thinner the air, the longer things keep going.</p>
<p>So imagine taking the last step. Remove the friction completely. Remove the air. Remove anything that could touch the book at all. What would it do after your shove? There is nothing left to slow it down, so there is no reason for it to slow down. It would keep moving at exactly the speed you gave it, in exactly the direction you pushed, for ever.</p>
<p>That thought experiment is the heart of Newton's first law. Motion does not need a cause to continue. Motion only needs a cause to <strong>change</strong>. A force can start something moving, stop it, speed it up, slow it down or turn it, but a moving thing with no force on it simply carries on. Physicists call this stubbornness <dfn title="An object's resistance to any change in its motion">inertia</dfn>, from the Latin word for idleness or laziness. An object, left alone, is too "lazy" to change what it is doing.</p>
</div>
<div class="wide">
<figure><div class="photo r219" data-img="The_station_pictured_from_the_SpaceX_Crew_Dragon_5.jpg" data-scene="scene-learn"></div><figcaption><b>The International Space Station.</b> It moves at almost 28 000 km/h. Nothing pushes it forward: it keeps the speed it was given, and the Earth's gravity bends its straight path into a circle.</figcaption></figure>
</div>
<div class="read">
<p>It is worth noticing how strange this is. On Earth we never see the first law in its pure form, because friction and air are everywhere. That is exactly why it took humanity so long to discover. The law describes a world without friction, and nobody had ever lived in one. Today we have: astronauts on the International Space Station see the first law every day, as a pen let go in mid air drifts slowly and steadily across the cabin until it bumps into a wall.</p>
</div>

## The law
> One sentence in words, one line in maths. Both say the same thing.

<div class="read">
<p>In 1687 Isaac Newton opened his great book, the <em>Principia</em>, with three laws of motion. The first one, in the English translation published in 1729, reads:</p>
</div>
<div class="pull"><blockquote>Every body perseveres in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a right line, unless it is compelled to change that state by forces impressed thereon.</blockquote><cite>Isaac Newton, Principia, 1687</cite></div>
<div class="read">
<p>In today's words: an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion keeps moving in a straight line at constant speed, unless a <dfn title="All the forces on an object added together, taking their directions into account">net force</dfn> acts on it. Two details in that sentence matter a lot.</p>
<p>The first is <strong>"in a straight line"</strong>. The law is about velocity, which means speed <em>and</em> direction together. Turning a corner at a steady 50 km/h is still a change of velocity, so it needs a force, even though the speedometer never moves. That is why you feel pushed sideways in a car going round a bend: your body is trying to keep going straight, and the car is forcing it to turn.</p>
<p>The second is <strong>"net"</strong>. Objects almost always have several forces on them at once. A book lying on a table is pulled down by gravity and pushed up by the table. A car cruising on a motorway is pushed forward by its engine and held back by air and road friction. If all the forces cancel each other out, the net force is zero, and the object behaves exactly as if there were no forces at all: it keeps its velocity. The book stays still. The car keeps cruising at the same speed.</p>
</div>
<div class="wide">
<div class="formula">
<div class="big">
<div class="math">ΣF = 0 <i>⟹</i> Δv = 0</div>
<p>If the forces on an object add up to zero, its velocity does not change. It can be zero, the object stays still, or it can be any other value, the object keeps moving exactly as it is.</p>
</div>
<div class="keys">
<h4>What the symbols mean</h4>
<dl>
<dt>Σ</dt><dd><b>"The sum of"</b>A Greek capital sigma. It means: add everything up.</dd>
<dt>F</dt><dd><b>Force</b>A push or a pull, measured in newtons (N). One newton is about the weight of a small apple.</dd>
<dt>v</dt><dd><b>Velocity</b>Speed in a given direction, in metres per second (m/s).</dd>
<dt>Δ</dt><dd><b>"The change in"</b>A Greek capital delta. Δv is how much the velocity changes.</dd>
<dt>⟹</dt><dd><b>"Which means that"</b>The left side leads to the right side.</dd>
</dl>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="read">
<p>Notice that rest and steady motion are treated as the same thing. To Newton there is no fundamental difference between a book lying on a table and a book gliding across frictionless ice at a constant speed: in both cases nothing is changing, and in both cases the net force is zero. Being still is simply moving at a speed of zero.</p>
<details class="deeper"><summary>Go deeper: still compared to what?</summary><div class="in">
<p>A cup on the table of a train is at rest for you, sitting opposite it. For someone standing on the platform as the train goes by, the same cup is moving at 100 km/h. Who is right? Both of you. Speed is always measured compared to something, and the first law works for both observers, as long as the train is moving steadily.</p>
<p>Viewpoints that are not speeding up, slowing down or turning are called <dfn title="A point of view that is not accelerating or rotating">inertial frames</dfn>, and Newton's laws hold in all of them. When the train brakes, the cup slides forward along the table. Nothing pushed it. The train stopped carrying it, and the cup simply kept the velocity it had.</p>
</div></details>
</div>

> A box on a field, no friction, and forces you control. Three experiments to run.

You can add forces to the box, choose how strong each one is and how long it pushes, and change the box's mass. Arrows above the box show its velocity and the forces on it, and a panel shows the numbers as time runs.</p>
<div class="exp"><span class="n">1</span><div><h4>A short push</h4><p>Add one force of 20 N to the right and let it act for one second. Then remove it and keep watching the velocity arrow.</p><details><summary>See what happens</summary><p>While the force acts, the box speeds up. <div class="exp"><span class="n">2</span><div><h4>A heavier box</h4><p>Double the mass and give it the same 20 N push for one second. ## Worked examples
> The law in action, step by step, with real numbers.

<div class="read">
<h3 style="margin-top:0">A puck on perfect ice</h3>
<p>A hockey puck of 0.17 kg slides across ice at 10 m/s. Imagine the ice is perfectly smooth and there is no air. How fast is the puck moving three seconds later?</p>
<p>Start with the forces. Gravity pulls the puck down with about 1.7 N. The ice pushes it up with exactly the same 1.7 N, otherwise the puck would sink into the ice or jump off it. There is nothing pushing it forwards or backwards. Add everything up and the net force is zero. The first law then gives the answer straight away: no net force, no change in velocity. The puck is still moving at 10 m/s, in the same direction. In those three seconds it has travelled 30 metres, and it will travel another 10 metres every second after that.</p>
<p>Notice that we did not need the puck's mass or the time to get the answer. That is the power of the first law: if the forces cancel, nothing else matters.</p>
<h3>The bus that brakes</h3>
<p>You are standing in a bus travelling at 50 km/h, about 14 m/s, and you are not holding on. The driver brakes hard. What happens to you?</p>
<p>The brakes put a force on the bus, through the wheels and the road. They do not put a force on you. Your feet are on the floor, but the friction between your shoes and the floor is small compared to the force needed to stop a whole person quickly. So, for a moment, there is almost no net force on your body, and your body does what the first law says: it keeps moving forwards at 14 m/s, while the bus around you slows down. To you it feels as if you are thrown forwards. In fact you are the only thing in the bus that is not changing speed.</p>
<div class="aside"><h4>Why buses have handles</h4><p>Holding a handle lets the bus pull on you, so you slow down together with it.</p></div>
<h3>Thirty years without an engine</h3>
<p>The Voyager 1 space probe is moving away from the Sun at about 17 km/s. Its rocket stages fell away in 1977, and since then it has only fired tiny thrusters to point its antenna at the Earth. Why has it not slowed to a stop in all the decades since?</p>
<p>Out between the stars there is almost no gas and no dust: no friction, no air. The Sun's gravity still pulls back on the probe, which is why it is a little slower today than it was when it left Saturn, but that pull grows weaker the further Voyager goes. With almost nothing acting on it, the first law takes over, and the probe keeps almost all of its speed. It does not need fuel to keep going. It would need fuel to stop.</p>
</div>

## In everyday life
> Once you know the first law, you start to see it everywhere.

<div class="read">
<h3 style="margin-top:0">Seat belts and headrests</h3>
<p>In a crash, a car can go from 50 km/h to zero in a fraction of a second. Everything not fixed to the car, including the people inside, keeps moving at 50 km/h until something stops it. A seat belt is that something: it applies the force to your hips and chest, the strongest parts of your body, and spreads the stop over a little time. The headrest does the opposite job in a crash from behind. The car is shoved forwards, your body goes with the seat, and your head, which nothing is pushing, would stay where it was and snap backwards. The headrest pushes it along with the rest of you.</p>
<h3>The tablecloth trick</h3>
<p>Magicians pull a tablecloth out from under plates and glasses without breaking anything. The trick is speed. The cloth rubs on the dishes for only a fraction of a second, so the friction force acts for too short a time to change their velocity much. The dishes were at rest, and they stay almost exactly at rest, while the cloth slides away underneath them.</p>
</div>

<div class="read">
<h3>Ketchup, hammers and wet dogs</h3>
<p>When you shake a ketchup bottle downwards and stop it suddenly, the bottle stops, but the ketchup inside keeps moving and slides out. A hammer head that has worked loose can be tightened by banging the end of the handle on the floor: the handle stops, the heavy head keeps moving down and wedges itself on tighter. And when a wet dog shakes itself, its skin changes direction many times a second, while the water drops keep flying straight on, away from the fur.</p>
<p>All of these are the same law. Something is moving, or still. Something else changes suddenly. And the first thing carries on as it was, because nothing has acted on it yet.</p>
</div>

## In space
> Space is the one place where the first law works almost perfectly.

<div class="read">
<p>On Earth friction is everywhere, so the first law always hides behind it. Space is different. Above the atmosphere there is almost nothing to rub against, and the first law stops being a thought experiment and becomes the most important rule of space flight.</p>
<h3>Coasting between planets</h3>
<p>A spacecraft on its way to Mars does not run its engines the whole way. It fires them for a few minutes to reach the right speed and direction, then switches them off and coasts for months. Engineers call this a coast phase, and it is the first law at work: once the spacecraft has its velocity, it keeps it, apart from the slow, predictable bending of its path by the gravity of the Sun and the planets.</p>
</div>
<div class="wide">
<figure><div class="photo r219 contain" data-img="Pale_Blue_Dot.png" data-scene="scene-learn"></div><figcaption><b>The Pale Blue Dot.</b> Voyager 1 took this photograph in 1990, from about 6 billion kilometres away. The Earth is the tiny speck in the band of light. The probe was coasting, as it still is today.</figcaption></figure>
</div>
<div class="read">
<h3>Why astronauts float</h3>
<p>Astronauts on the International Space Station are not floating because there is no gravity up there. Gravity at the station's height is still about 90% as strong as on the ground. They float because the station and everything in it are falling around the Earth together. Inside that falling station, the first law is easy to see: a tool let go in mid air hangs there, and a gentle push sends it drifting across the cabin at a slow, steady speed until it hits a wall.</p>
<h3>A danger as well</h3>
<p>The first law has a dark side in orbit. A fleck of paint or a lost bolt keeps the orbital speed it had, around 7 to 8 km/s, for years. At that speed a piece of debris the size of a marble hits with the energy of a falling piano. Spacecraft carry shields for small debris, and the space station sometimes fires its engines to move out of the path of larger pieces.</p>
</div>

## How we found out
> For two thousand years the smartest people alive believed the opposite.

<div class="wide">
<div class="timeline-h">
<div><b>≈ 350 BC</b><h4>Aristotle</h4><p>Moving things need a mover. Without a push they stop.</p></div>
<div><b>500s</b><h4>John Philoponus</h4><p>A thrower gives a stone an inner "impetus".</p></div>
<div><b>1300s</b><h4>Jean Buridan</h4><p>Impetus would last for ever if nothing resisted it.</p></div>
<div><b>1638</b><h4>Galileo</h4><p>A ball on a flat, smooth plane would roll for ever.</p></div>
<div><b>1644</b><h4>Descartes</h4><p>Motion continues in a straight line.</p></div>
<div><b>1687</b><h4>Newton</h4><p>Writes it as the first law of motion.</p></div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="read" style="margin-top:56px">
<p>The Greek philosopher Aristotle, writing around 350 BC, divided motion into two kinds. Natural motion was a thing returning to its proper place: a stone falling down, smoke rising up. Every other motion was "violent", and it needed a mover, something pushing all the time. Take away the mover and the motion would stop. This matched everyday experience perfectly, and for almost two thousand years it was what educated people believed.</p>
<p>There was one awkward problem. A thrown stone keeps flying after it leaves your hand. What is pushing it? Aristotle's answer was that the air rushing in behind the stone kept shoving it along. Even in ancient times many people found this hard to believe. In the 500s the scholar John Philoponus argued that the thrower gives the stone something of its own, a kind of inner motion, which slowly wears off. Eight hundred years later in Paris, Jean Buridan named this <em>impetus</em> and took a bold step: if nothing resisted it, he said, the impetus would never wear off at all.</p>
</div>
<div class="wide">
<figure><div class="photo r219" data-img="Portrait_of_Sir_Isaac_Newton%2C_1689_%28brightened%29.jpg" data-scene="scene-learn"></div><figcaption><b>Isaac Newton in 1689,</b> two years after the <em>Principia</em> was published. He was 46.</figcaption></figure>
</div>
<div class="read">
<p>The experiment that changed everything was Galileo Galilei's, in early-1600s Italy. He rolled balls down one ramp and up another. With smooth ramps, the ball climbed almost back to the height it started from. When he made the second ramp less steep, the ball rolled further along it to reach that height. So what if the second ramp were perfectly flat? The ball could never reach its starting height, so it would roll on, he reasoned, for ever. Galileo published the idea in 1638 in his last book, <em>Two New Sciences</em>, written while he was under house arrest.</p>
<p>Galileo still thought this endless motion would follow the curve of the Earth. The French philosopher René Descartes made it a straight line in 1644. Then Isaac Newton, in 1687, did something nobody had done before: he made it the first of three laws that, together with his law of gravity, could predict the motion of everything from a falling apple to the Moon. The idea that nothing needs to keep pushing a moving object was the foundation all of it was built on.</p>
</div>

## Common mistakes
> Even people who know the law well slip back into Aristotle's way of thinking.

<div class="read">
<div class="myth"><div class="no"><span>People often think</span><p>A moving object needs a force to keep it moving.</p></div><div class="yes"><span>What really happens</span><p>A force is needed only to change the motion. A car on a motorway needs its engine only to cancel the friction and air resistance that would otherwise slow it.</p></div></div>
<div class="myth"><div class="no"><span>People often think</span><p>If an object is not moving, there are no forces on it.</p></div><div class="yes"><span>What really happens</span><p>There can be large forces that cancel out. A bridge carrying traffic is pushed and pulled in many directions at once, and stays still because they add up to zero.</p></div></div>
<div class="myth"><div class="no"><span>People often think</span><p>Heavy things keep moving because they have more force inside them.</p></div><div class="yes"><span>What really happens</span><p>Objects do not carry forces around. Heavy objects have more inertia, so they are harder to start and harder to stop, but a light object with no force on it keeps moving just as surely.</p></div></div>
<div class="myth"><div class="no"><span>People often think</span><p>In space there is no gravity, which is why the first law works there.</p></div><div class="yes"><span>What really happens</span><p>Gravity reaches everywhere. The first law works so clearly in space because there is no friction, and orbits are simply straight lines bent by gravity.</p></div></div>
<p style="margin-top:40px">Every one of these mistakes comes from the same place: on Earth, friction is so common that we stop noticing it is a force at all. Keep asking "what is pushing on this, and do the pushes cancel?" and the first law will rarely surprise you again.</p>
