When one object pushes on another, the second pushes back on the first just as hard, in the opposite direction: forces always come in pairs.

## The idea
> You cannot push something without being pushed back.

<div class="read">
<p>Stand on a skateboard and push against a wall. You do not move the wall, but you roll backwards. Something pushed you, and the only thing you touched was the wall. The wall pushed you back.</p>
<p>Now stand on skates opposite a friend, both of you still, and push each other's hands. You both roll away, in opposite directions. It does not matter who "did the pushing". Even if only you straightened your arms, you both moved. A push is never one-way.</p>
<p>That is the third law. Forces are not things one object does to another. They are something that happens <strong>between</strong> two objects, and they always come as a pair: one on each object, equal in size, opposite in direction. You cannot touch without being touched.</p>
<div class="aside"><h4>Try it at home</h4><p>Blow up a balloon, hold the end shut, then let it go. The balloon pushes the air out backwards, and the air pushes the balloon forwards.</p></div>
</div>

## The law
> One sentence in words, one line in maths. Both say the same thing.

<div class="read">
<p>Newton's third law, in the English translation of the <em>Principia</em> published in 1729, reads:</p>
</div>
<div class="pull"><blockquote>To every action there is always opposed an equal reaction: or the mutual actions of two bodies upon each other are always equal, and directed to contrary parts.</blockquote><cite>Isaac Newton, Principia, 1687</cite></div>
<div class="read">
<p>In today's words: if object A pushes or pulls on object B, then B pushes or pulls on A with a force of exactly the same size, in exactly the opposite direction. The two forces appear and disappear together, at the same instant.</p>
<p>The pair is always of the same kind. If A pulls B with gravity, B pulls A with gravity. If A presses on B by touching it, B presses back by touching. The Earth pulls you down with your weight, and you pull the Earth up with exactly the same force. The Earth does not seem to move because its mass is enormous: by the second law, the same force gives it an acceleration far too small to notice.</p>
</div>
<div class="wide">
<div class="formula">
<div class="big">
<div class="math">F<sub>A→B</sub> = −F<sub>B→A</sub></div>
<p>The force that A puts on B is equal and opposite to the force that B puts on A. The minus sign means "the opposite direction".</p>
</div>
<div class="keys">
<h4>What the symbols mean</h4>
<dl>
<dt>F<sub>A→B</sub></dt><dd><b>The force of A on B</b>How hard A pushes or pulls on B, in newtons (N), and in which direction.</dd>
<dt>F<sub>B→A</sub></dt><dd><b>The force of B on A</b>The push back, on the other object.</dd>
<dt>−</dt><dd><b>"The opposite way"</b>Same size, opposite direction.</dd>
</dl>
</div>
</div>
</div>
<div class="read">
<details class="deeper"><summary>Go deeper: if they are equal and opposite, why don't they cancel?</summary><div class="in">
<p>This is the most common puzzle about the third law. Forces only cancel when they act on the <strong>same</strong> object. The two forces of a third-law pair always act on <strong>different</strong> objects: one on A, one on B.</p>
<p>When you push your friend on skates, the force on your friend makes your friend move, and the force on you makes you move. Neither force can cancel the other, because each is looking after a different body. To find out how one object moves, you add up only the forces acting on that object.</p>
</div></details>
<details class="deeper"><summary>Go deeper: momentum is kept</summary><div class="in">
<p>The two forces of a pair are equal and act for the same time, so they change the <strong>momentum</strong> (mass times velocity) of the two objects by equal and opposite amounts. Whatever one object gains, the other loses.</p>
<p>This means that, if nothing outside pushes on them, the total momentum of the two objects never changes. Two skaters standing still have a total momentum of zero. After they push apart, one has momentum to the left and the other the same amount to the right, and the total is still zero. This rule, the conservation of momentum, is one of the most important in all of physics.</p>
</div></details>
</div>

## Worked examples
> The law in action, step by step, with real numbers.

<div class="read">
<h3 style="margin-top:0">Two skaters push apart</h3>
<p>A skater of 40 kg and a skater of 80 kg stand still on ice and push each other. The lighter one moves away at 2 m/s. How fast does the heavier one move?</p>
<p>Before the push the total momentum is zero. After the push it must still be zero. The light skater has a momentum of 40 · 2 = 80 kg·m/s. So the heavy skater must have 80 kg·m/s in the other direction, which means a speed of 80 / 80 = 1 m/s. Twice the mass, half the speed. Both felt exactly the same force, for exactly the same time.</p>
<h3>An astronaut throws a wrench</h3>
<p>An astronaut of 100 kg, floating still outside a space station, throws a 2 kg wrench away at 5 m/s. What happens to the astronaut?</p>
<p>The wrench gets a momentum of 2 · 5 = 10 kg·m/s. The astronaut gets the same amount, the other way: 10 / 100 = 0.1 m/s. That is slow, but there is nothing in space to stop it. After a minute the astronaut has drifted six metres. This is why astronauts are always tied to the station.</p>
<h3>The box on the table</h3>
<p>A book lies on a table. Gravity pulls it down, the table pushes it up. Are these two forces a third-law pair?</p>
<p>No, and this is a trap. Both forces act on the same object, the book, so they cannot be a pair. They are equal because the book is not moving (that is the first law). The true third-law partner of the table's push on the book is the book's push on the table. The partner of the Earth's pull on the book is the book's pull on the Earth.</p>
</div>

## In everyday life
> Walking, swimming and driving all work by pushing something backwards.

<div class="read">
<p>When you walk, your foot pushes backwards on the ground. The ground pushes forwards on your foot, and that forward push is what moves you. Try walking on smooth ice, where your foot cannot push back on the ground, and you go nowhere.</p>
<p>A swimmer pushes water backwards with hands and feet, and the water pushes the swimmer forwards. A boat's propeller pushes water backwards. A car's tyres push backwards on the road, and the road pushes the car forwards. In every case, to go forwards you have to push something else backwards.</p>
<p>Firing a gun makes it kick back into the shooter's shoulder: the gun pushes the bullet forwards and the bullet pushes the gun backwards. A garden hose spraying hard pulls back in your hands for the same reason. Even a jumping person pushes the whole Earth away a tiny amount.</p>
</div>

## In space
> How a rocket moves when there is nothing to push against.

<div class="read">
<p>A common question is how a rocket can move in space, where there is no air or ground to push on. The answer is that it does not need any. A rocket carries what it pushes against: its own fuel.</p>
<p>In the engine, fuel burns and turns into very hot gas, which is pushed out of the nozzle at several kilometres per second. The engine pushes the gas backwards, and the gas pushes the engine, and the whole rocket, forwards. The air around a rocket at launch does not help it at all. In fact it gets in the way: rocket engines work slightly better in empty space than at sea level.</p>
<p>The same law turns spacecraft around. Small thrusters puff gas in one direction to rotate the craft the other way. And when the Apollo astronauts left the Moon, the top half of their lander pushed against the bottom half, which stayed behind on the surface.</p>
</div>

## How we found out
> From cannons kicking back to the conservation of momentum.

<div class="read">
<div class="timeline-h">
<div><b>1600s</b><h4>Recoil</h4><p>Gunners know that a cannon jumps back when it fires.</p></div>
<div><b>1668</b><h4>Wallis, Wren, Huygens</h4><p>Study colliding balls and find that the total momentum is kept.</p></div>
<div><b>1687</b><h4>Newton</h4><p>Makes "action and reaction" the third of his laws of motion.</p></div>
<div><b>1903</b><h4>Tsiolkovsky</h4><p>Shows that a rocket can reach space by throwing its exhaust backwards.</p></div>
<div><b>1920</b><h4>The New York Times</h4><p>Claims a rocket cannot work in empty space. It apologises in 1969.</p></div>
<div><b>1926</b><h4>Goddard</h4><p>Launches the first liquid-fuelled rocket.</p></div>
</div>
<p>People felt the third law long before it had a name. Every gunner knew the recoil of a cannon, and every sailor knew that pushing off from a dock pushes the boat away. In 1668 the Royal Society in London asked for papers on collisions, and John Wallis, Christopher Wren and Christiaan Huygens all showed, in different ways, that when two balls collide the total "quantity of motion" stays the same.</p>
<p>Newton saw that this follows from one simple idea: in every interaction, the two forces are equal and opposite. He made it his third law, and used it to show that even the Sun is pulled a little by every planet.</p>
<p>The law was still misunderstood more than two centuries later. In 1920 a newspaper wrote that the rocket pioneer Robert Goddard did not understand that a rocket needs air to push against. On 17 July 1969, as Apollo 11 was on its way to the Moon, the same newspaper printed a short correction.</p>
</div>

## Common mistakes
> Three ideas that sound right and are not.

<div class="read">
<div class="myth"><div class="no"><span>Not true</span><p>The bigger or faster object pushes harder.</p></div><div class="yes"><span>What really happens</span><p>Both forces are always exactly equal. A lorry hitting a fly pushes on the fly exactly as hard as the fly pushes on the lorry. The fly just has much less mass to absorb it.</p></div></div>
<div class="myth"><div class="no"><span>Not true</span><p>Action and reaction cancel, so nothing can ever move.</p></div><div class="yes"><span>What really happens</span><p>They act on different objects, so they never cancel each other. Each one moves its own object.</p></div></div>
<div class="myth"><div class="no"><span>Not true</span><p>A rocket needs air to push against.</p></div><div class="yes"><span>What really happens</span><p>A rocket pushes on its own exhaust gas. It works best in empty space, where there is no air in the way.</p></div></div>
</div>
