## The object
> A black hole four million times heavier than the Sun, at the centre of our galaxy.

<div class="read">
<p>Sagittarius A*, said "Sagittarius A star" and often written Sgr A*, is the supermassive black hole at the centre of the Milky Way. It has a mass of about 4.3 million Suns, packed into a region smaller than the orbit of Mercury. Everything in our galaxy, including the Sun and its planets, goes around it.</p>
<p>A black hole is a place where so much mass is squeezed into so small a space that its gravity lets nothing escape, not even light. Its edge is called the event horizon. For Sagittarius A* the event horizon is about 25 million kilometres across, roughly seventeen times the width of the Sun.</p>
<p>It is about 26,700 light-years away, in the direction of the constellation Sagittarius. We cannot see it with our eyes, or with ordinary telescopes: the centre of the galaxy is hidden behind thick clouds of dust. It was found with radio waves, and studied with infrared light and X-rays, which pass through the dust.</p>
</div>

## Finding it
> In 1974, a faint, tiny radio source at the heart of the galaxy.

<div class="read">
<p>Radio astronomers had known since the 1930s that radio waves come from the direction of the centre of the galaxy. The bright region there was named Sagittarius A. But it was a big, messy area of gas, and nobody knew what was at its very centre.</p>
<p>In February 1974, the American astronomers Bruce Balick and Robert Brown used two radio telescopes in West Virginia, working together as one larger telescope, to look at it in more detail. Inside Sagittarius A they found something new: a single point of radio waves, extremely small and bright. Brown later named it Sagittarius A*, with the star meaning "the exciting one".</p>
<p>Several astronomers had already suggested that galaxies might have huge black holes at their centres. The tiny radio source was exactly what such a black hole, slowly eating gas, would look like. But a point of radio waves is not proof. To prove it, someone had to weigh it.</p>
</div>

## The stars around it
> Watching stars race around an invisible object for thirty years.

<div class="read">
<p>The way to weigh something you cannot see is to watch things moving around it. The faster they move and the closer they are, the heavier the object they orbit. That is how we know the mass of the Sun: from the speed of the planets.</p>
<p>From the early 1990s, two teams started watching the stars close to the centre of the galaxy in infrared light. One, in Germany, was led by Reinhard Genzel and used telescopes in Chile. The other, in the United States, was led by Andrea Ghez and used the Keck telescopes in Hawaii. Year after year they measured the stars' positions, using new methods to remove the blurring caused by the Earth's air.</p>
<p>The stars were moving astonishingly fast, along ellipses, around a single invisible point exactly where Sagittarius A* is. One star, called S2, goes all the way around in about sixteen years. At its closest, it passes only about 120 times the Earth–Sun distance from the centre, at more than 7,000 kilometres per second.</p>
<details class="deeper"><summary>Go deeper: how the orbit gives the mass</summary><div class="in">
<p>For anything in orbit, Kepler's third law links the size of the orbit, the time it takes, and the mass at the centre. If you know how big the ellipse is and how long one orbit takes, you can calculate the mass that is pulling the star around.</p>
<p>For S2 the answer is about 4 million times the mass of the Sun. And because S2 comes so close, all of that mass must fit inside its orbit. Nothing except a black hole can be so heavy and so small at the same time.</p>
</div></details>
</div>

## Weighing it
> Four million Suns in a space smaller than the orbit of Mercury.

<div class="read">
<p>The stars' orbits showed that about 4.3 million Suns' worth of mass is packed into a tiny region at the centre of the galaxy. No cluster of stars could be squeezed so tightly without collapsing. The only explanation that fits is a supermassive black hole.</p>
<p>In 2018, when S2 made its closest pass, the German team measured something else: as the star dived deep into the black hole's gravity, its light was stretched slightly towards red, exactly by the amount Einstein's theory of gravity predicts. Later they saw the whole orbit slowly turning, another prediction of the theory.</p>
<p>In 2020 Andrea Ghez and Reinhard Genzel shared the Nobel Prize in Physics for discovering Sagittarius A*'s nature, together with Roger Penrose, who had shown in theory that black holes are a natural result of Einstein's gravity.</p>
</div>

## The picture
> In 2022, the first image of the black hole at the centre of our galaxy.

<div class="read">
<p>A black hole itself is black, but the gas swirling around it glows. Just outside the event horizon, the black hole's gravity bends that light into a bright ring around a dark centre, its "shadow".</p>
<p>Seeing that ring from the Earth is like seeing a doughnut on the Moon. No single telescope is big enough, so the Event Horizon Telescope joined radio dishes all over the world, from Hawaii to Spain to the South Pole, into one telescope the size of the Earth. They observed together in April 2017, and the data was combined using atomic clocks.</p>
<p>The gas around Sagittarius A* moves so fast that it changes in minutes, which made the picture much harder to make than the first black hole image, of the galaxy M87, released in 2019. On 12 May 2022 the team finally showed it: a glowing orange ring around a dark centre, exactly the size Einstein's theory predicts for a black hole of 4 million Suns.</p>
</div>

## A quiet giant
> Our black hole eats very little, and flickers.

<div class="read">
<p>Some supermassive black holes are surrounded by so much falling gas that they outshine their whole galaxy. Sagittarius A* is the opposite. It is one of the faintest known for its size, because very little gas falls into it.</p>
<p>It is not completely still. Several times a day it flares, getting brighter in infrared light and X-rays for an hour or so, probably when small clumps of gas are heated close to the horizon. Telescopes such as the Chandra X-ray Observatory watch these flares to learn how matter behaves just before it falls in.</p>
<p>There are also signs of a busier past. Huge bubbles of hot gas, called the Fermi bubbles, stretch tens of thousands of light-years above and below the centre of the galaxy. They may have been blown by an outburst of the black hole a few million years ago.</p>
</div>

## Is it dangerous?
> No. It is very far away, and it is not coming closer.

<div class="read">
<p>Black holes do not suck things in from a distance. From far away, a black hole pulls just like any other object of the same mass. The Sun goes around the centre of the galaxy in a wide, calm orbit, about 26,700 light-years away, taking about 230 million years for one lap. It has done so safely for billions of years.</p>
<p>Only things that come very close, such as the stars like S2 or clouds of gas that wander near the centre, are in any danger.</p>
</div>
