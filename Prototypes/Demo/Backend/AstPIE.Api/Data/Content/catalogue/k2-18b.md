## The planet
> Bigger than Earth, smaller than Neptune, and unlike anything in our Solar System.

<div class="read">
<p>Our Solar System has small rocky planets like the Earth and Mars, and giant planets like Jupiter and Neptune, with a big empty gap in between. Nothing orbits the Sun that is two or three times the width of the Earth. Yet when astronomers began finding planets around other stars, this in-between size turned out to be the most common of all. They call these worlds <dfn title="Planets bigger than Earth but smaller than Neptune, usually with a thick layer of gas">sub-Neptunes</dfn>, and K2-18b is one of the most famous.</p>
<p>It circles a small, cool star called K2-18, in the constellation Leo, once every 33 days. That is a very short year, because it orbits much closer to its star than the Earth does to the Sun. But K2-18 is far dimmer than the Sun, so even that close, the planet receives roughly the same amount of starlight as the Earth. It sits inside its star's <dfn title="The range of distances from a star where a planet could have liquid water on its surface">habitable zone</dfn>, the band of orbits where liquid water could exist.</p>
<p>That single fact made K2-18b a celebrity. It is one of very few planets in a habitable zone whose atmosphere we can actually study, and every few years a new observation has made headlines, from water vapour in 2019 to a possible sign of life in 2025. Each claim has been argued over fiercely. To understand why, we have to start with how anyone knows anything about a planet that nobody can see.</p>
</div>

## Finding it
> A broken space telescope, a second chance, and a star that dimmed every 33 days.

<div class="wide"><figure><div class="photo r219 contain" data-img="Kepler_Space_Telescope_spacecraft_model_2.png" data-scene="scene-catalogue"></div><figcaption><b>The Kepler space telescope.</b> It stared at stars for nine years and found more than 2 600 planets.</figcaption></figure></div>
<div class="read">
<p>NASA's Kepler space telescope was launched in 2009 with one job: to stare at the same patch of sky, watching about 150 000 stars at once, and catch the tiny dips in brightness that happen when a planet passes in front of its star. In 2013 the second of its four reaction wheels, the spinning wheels that kept it pointing steadily, broke down. The mission seemed to be over.</p>
<p>Engineers found a clever rescue. By pointing Kepler so that the pressure of sunlight pushed evenly on its body, they could hold it steady for about 80 days at a time, looking at a new strip of sky along the ecliptic each time. The new mission was called K2. In its very first observing campaign, in 2014, it watched the star K2-18 and caught it dimming by about 0.3% for a few hours. Then again. And again, every 33 days.</p>
<p>The discovery was published in 2015. A single dip could be anything, but repeating dips at exactly the same depth and interval are the signature of a planet passing in front of its star. NASA's Spitzer space telescope then caught another transit in infrared light, confirming that the planet was real and pinning down its size.</p>
<details class="deeper"><summary>Go deeper: how big a dip in starlight?</summary><div class="in">
<p>When a planet crosses its star, it blocks a share of the light equal to its area divided by the star's area. Because area goes with the square of the width:</p>
<div class="eq">dip = (R planet ÷ R star)² = (16 600 km ÷ 306 000 km)² ≈ 0.3%</div>
<p>Work backwards, and the depth of the dip tells you the size of the planet, as long as you know the size of the star. For the Earth crossing the Sun the dip would be only 0.008%, which is why small planets around big stars are so hard to find.</p>
</div></details>
</div>

<div class="read">
<p>K2-18 is a red dwarf, a type of star known to astronomers as an M dwarf. It has about half the mass of the Sun and a little less than half its width, and its surface is around 3 500 degrees, compared with the Sun's 5 800. That makes it glow a deep orange-red and shine at only a few percent of the Sun's brightness. It is far too faint to see without a telescope.</p>
<p>Red dwarfs are the most common stars in the Milky Way: about three out of every four stars are one. They burn their fuel so slowly that they can shine for hundreds of billions of years, far longer than the universe has existed. That makes them attractive places to look for life, because their planets have plenty of time. But they also have a darker side. Young red dwarfs often throw off powerful flares that could strip the air from nearby planets.</p>
<p>K2-18b is not alone. Careful measurements of how the star wobbles revealed a second planet, K2-18c, closer in, with an orbit of about 9 days and at least five times the mass of the Earth. It does not pass in front of the star as seen from here, so we have never seen it dim the light. We know it exists only from the tug it gives its star.</p>
<p>Because K2-18b orbits so close to its star, it is probably <dfn title="When a planet always keeps the same side facing its star, the way the Moon always shows the same face to the Earth">tidally locked</dfn>: one side always faces the star in permanent daylight, and the other side is always in night. If it has an ocean, the winds and currents moving heat between the two sides would shape its whole climate.</p>
</div>

<div class="read">
<p>We know K2-18b's width from the transit: about 2.6 times the width of the Earth, roughly 33 000 kilometres. We know its mass from a completely different measurement. As the planet circles its star, it pulls the star back and forth a tiny amount, a few metres per second, and that wobble shows up as a slight shift in the colour of the starlight. In 2017 and 2019 astronomers used the HARPS spectrograph in Chile to measure it, and found the planet has about 8.6 times the mass of the Earth.</p>
<p>Put the two together and you get the planet's density, and that is where it gets interesting. If K2-18b were made of rock and iron like the Earth, something 2.6 times as wide would weigh much more, somewhere around 20 to 30 Earths. Instead it is only about half as dense as our planet. So it cannot be solid rock all the way through. It must be wrapped in a deep layer of something light: water, hydrogen gas, or both.</p>
</div>

## Its air
> Starlight that passes through the planet's atmosphere carries a fingerprint of what it is made of.

<div class="read">
<p>Every time K2-18b passes in front of its star, a thin ring of starlight shines through the edge of its atmosphere on the way to us. Each gas absorbs light at its own particular colours, so by comparing the starlight during a transit with the starlight outside one, astronomers can read which gases are there. It is called transmission spectroscopy, and it is one of the hardest measurements in astronomy: the effect is a change of a few hundredths of a percent.</p>
<p>In 2019 two teams used old observations from the Hubble Space Telescope and announced that they had found water vapour. It was the first time water had been detected in the atmosphere of a planet in a habitable zone, and it made headlines around the world, often with the words "possibly habitable". Later work showed that some of that signal might come from methane or from the star itself, and the story became more complicated.</p>
</div>
<div class="wide"><figure><div class="photo r219 contain" data-img="JWST_spacecraft_model_3.png" data-scene="scene-catalogue"></div><figcaption><b>The James Webb Space Telescope.</b> Its huge gold mirror collects enough infrared light to read the atmospheres of planets like K2-18b.</figcaption></figure></div>
<div class="read">
<p>Then, in 2023, the James Webb Space Telescope looked. Its much bigger mirror and infrared instruments gave a far clearer picture, and here is what it has found so far.</p>
<div class="gases">
<div class="gas"><div><h4>Hydrogen</h4><span class="sure"><span class="dot" style="background:#6FCDBF"></span>Certain</span></div><p>The atmosphere is rich in hydrogen, the lightest gas of all. That explains how the planet can be so large and so light at the same time.</p></div>
<div class="gas"><div><h4>Methane</h4><span class="sure"><span class="dot" style="background:#6FCDBF"></span>Detected in 2023</span></div><p>About one percent of the air. On Earth, most methane is made by living things, but on a planet like this it can also form naturally.</p></div>
<div class="gas"><div><h4>Carbon dioxide</h4><span class="sure"><span class="dot" style="background:#6FCDBF"></span>Detected in 2023</span></div><p>Also about one percent. Seeing it together with methane, and with almost no ammonia, is what first made scientists think of an ocean.</p></div>
<div class="gas"><div><h4>Water vapour</h4><span class="sure"><span class="dot" style="background:#E2BD56"></span>Debated</span></div><p>Claimed in 2019, not clearly seen by Webb high in the atmosphere. If there is an ocean, the water may be trapped lower down, where it is too cold to rise.</p></div>
<div class="gas"><div><h4>Dimethyl sulfide</h4><span class="sure"><span class="dot" style="background:#E98373"></span>Not confirmed</span></div><p>On Earth this gas is made almost only by living things, mostly ocean plankton. A hint of it was reported in 2023 and a stronger claim in 2025. Independent teams re-analysing the data found the evidence much weaker.</p></div>
</div>
</div>

## Ocean or not
> Three very different worlds can explain the same faint starlight.

<div class="read">
<p>The data from Webb are real, but they can be explained in more than one way. That is why scientists still argue about what K2-18b actually is. There are three main ideas.</p>
</div>

<div class="read">
<p>The hycean idea was proposed in 2021 by Nikku Madhusudhan and his team at the University of Cambridge, and K2-18b became its best candidate. The strongest argument for it is the missing ammonia. A deep, hot hydrogen atmosphere should contain plenty of ammonia, but an ocean underneath would dissolve it, just as Webb seems to show. Other researchers answered that chemistry deep in a mini-Neptune, or in a magma ocean, could hide the ammonia too. Each model fits the data. Telling them apart needs better data.</p>
</div>

## Could it host life?
> Nobody knows. Here is what would have to be true.

<div class="read">
<p>For K2-18b to host life as we know it, at least three things would have to be true. It would need liquid water. It would need that water to be at a temperature where complex chemistry can happen, neither boiling nor frozen. And it would need a source of energy and the right chemical ingredients. If the hycean picture is right, the first two might be met in the upper layers of a warm ocean, lit by the red light of its star.</p>
<p>That is a big "if". Even on a hycean world the ocean could be too hot, or covered by such a thick atmosphere that the pressure at its surface is crushing. Its star's flares could have damaged the atmosphere in the past. And the gas that caused so much excitement, dimethyl sulfide, has not been confirmed. Scientists have also shown that some molecules once thought to be made only by life can form without it, for example on comets.</p>
<div class="pull" style="padding:0;margin:50px 0"><blockquote>Extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence. For K2-18b, that evidence is still being collected.</blockquote></div>
<p>What K2-18b has already done is just as important. It has turned a vague question, "is there life out there?", into precise measurements that telescopes can actually make. Whatever the answer turns out to be, the methods being tested on this one faint dot will be used on hundreds of planets to come.</p>
</div>

## The name
> Planet names look like codes. They can be read.

<div class="read">
<div class="name-fig">
<div><b>K2</b><span>The second mission of the Kepler space telescope, which found the planet.</span></div>
<div><b>18</b><span>The 18th star confirmed to have planets in the K2 mission's list.</span></div>
<div><b>b</b><span>The first planet found around it. The star itself counts as "a", so planets start at "b".</span></div>
</div>
<p>The same star also appears in other catalogues under other names. In the main list of targets for the K2 mission it is EPIC 201912552, so the planet is sometimes written as EPIC 201912552 b. The second planet, found later, became K2-18c. If a third is ever found, it will be K2-18d, whatever its distance from the star.</p>
</div>

## What comes next
> More telescope time, new missions, and a planet that will keep astronomers busy for years.

<div class="read">
<p>The James Webb Space Telescope will keep returning to K2-18b, each transit adding a little more signal to the faint fingerprint of its atmosphere. More transits mean clearer data, and clearer data could finally show whether dimethyl sulfide is there, whether water vapour is hiding lower down, and which of the three kinds of world the planet really is.</p>
<p>After Webb, the European Space Agency's Ariel mission, planned to launch in 2029, will be the first telescope built specifically to study the atmospheres of about a thousand planets around other stars. In the 2040s NASA hopes to launch the Habitable Worlds Observatory, designed to photograph Earth-like planets directly and search their light for signs of life.</p>
<p>K2-18b is unlikely to be the planet where we first find life beyond the Earth. But it may well be the planet where we learn how to look.</p>
</div>
