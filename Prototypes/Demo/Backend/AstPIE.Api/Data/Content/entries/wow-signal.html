=== The night | | Nobody was watching. A computer was listening, and printing what it heard.

Since 1973 Ohio State University's radio telescope, nicknamed Big Ear, had been doing something most astronomers thought was a long shot. It was searching the sky for signals from other civilisations, a field known as SETI, the Search for Extraterrestrial Intelligence. Night after night the telescope listened, and an IBM 1130 computer analysed what it heard and printed the results on long sheets of continuous paper.

At 22:16 local time on Monday 15 August 1977, the turning Earth swept Big Ear's view across a patch of sky in the constellation Sagittarius. For the next 72 seconds the computer recorded a signal that rose steadily, peaked, and faded away again. Then it was over. Nobody was in the control room to notice. The numbers were simply printed on the paper with all the others, and the paper was filed for someone to check later.

That someone was Jerry Ehman, an astronomer who volunteered to go through the printouts by hand. A few days later, sitting at his kitchen table, he reached the sheet from the night of 15 August. Among the usual columns of ones and twos, one column suddenly read 6, E, Q, U, J, 5. He circled it in red pen and wrote one word in the margin: Wow!

Among the usual columns of ones and twos, one column suddenly read 6EQUJ5.
=== The printout | | Six characters, one every twelve seconds. Here is how to read them.

The printout looks like a meaningless jumble, but it follows a simple code. Big Ear split the radio band it was listening to into 50 narrow channels, each 10 kilohertz wide, one column per channel. Every 12 seconds the computer printed one character for each channel, telling how strong the signal in that channel was compared to the normal background hiss of space.

A space meant the signal was weaker than the background. The numbers 1 to 9 meant the signal was that many times stronger. To fit stronger signals into a single character, the computer then switched to letters: A meant 10, B meant 11, and so on up to Z for 35. Almost everything on a normal printout was a 1 or a 2, the ordinary noise of the universe.

[[decoder]]

Read that way, 6EQUJ5 is the shape of a hill: 6, 14, 26, 30, 19, 5. The signal climbed, peaked at thirty times the background, and fell away again over exactly 72 seconds. And it appeared in only one of the 50 channels. Whatever made it was sending on a single, very narrow frequency.

That is what made Ehman write "Wow!". Natural sources in space, like glowing gas clouds or exploding stars, usually spread their radio waves across many frequencies at once. A signal squeezed into one narrow channel looks much more like a transmitter: the kind of signal a radio station, a radar, or someone deliberately trying to be heard would make.

=== The telescope | | A radio ear the size of three football fields, which never moved at all.

Big Ear did not look like the dish-shaped radio telescopes most people picture. It was designed by the radio astronomer John Kraus and built between 1956 and 1961 on a field near the town of Delaware, Ohio. A huge flat reflector, more than 100 metres long, could be tilted up or down. It sent incoming radio waves along a ground plane covered in aluminium, as large as three football fields, towards a fixed curved reflector at the far end, which focused them onto the receivers.

The telescope could not turn from side to side. Instead, it let the Earth do the work. As the planet rotates, the sky drifts past overhead, and any point in the sky takes a fixed time to cross the telescope's narrow view. For Big Ear that time was about 72 seconds. So a real signal from space should rise, peak and fall over exactly that long, which is precisely what the Wow! signal did.

There was one more detail, and it turned out to be the most frustrating one. Big Ear had two receiving horns side by side, pointing at slightly different parts of the sky. Any source in the sky should pass through the first horn and then, about three minutes later, through the second. The Wow! signal appeared in only one. Either the source switched off within those three minutes, or something stranger was going on. And because the data from the two horns were combined in a way that cannot now be untangled, nobody can even say which horn caught it.

Big Ear kept listening until 1998, when the land it stood on was sold and the telescope was taken apart to make room for a golf course.

=== Where it came from | | Two possible spots in the constellation of the Archer, and nothing obvious in either.

Because of the two horns, the signal has two possible positions, very close together, in the constellation Sagittarius, the Archer. Its brightest stars form a shape that looks like a teapot, and the Wow! signal came from a little to the north-west of it, near a group of faint stars called Chi Sagittarii. This is also the general direction of the centre of our galaxy, the most crowded part of the Milky Way.

For decades no particular star could be linked to the signal. Then, in 2022, the amateur astronomer Alberto Caballero searched star catalogues for stars like our Sun in the area. He found one, about 1 800 light years away, that fits: a star of the same type and temperature as the Sun, which in principle could have planets like the Earth. It has been suggested as a good target for future listening. So far, nothing has been heard from it.

1 800 light years

Even at the speed of light, a reply from Earth would take 1 800 years to arrive, and an answer as long again to come back.

=== The search | | Astronomers have pointed telescope after telescope at the same spot. The sky has stayed silent.

If the signal came from a transmitter, it might come again. Big Ear's team pointed the telescope at the same part of the sky more than a hundred times in the months and years that followed. They heard nothing.

Others joined the search with far more sensitive instruments. In the 1980s and 1990s the astronomer Robert Gray spent years on it, first with a telescope at Harvard University's Oak Ridge Observatory and then, in 1995 and 1996, with the Very Large Array in New Mexico, a group of 27 dishes that together are many times more sensitive than Big Ear. In 1999 he tried again with a radio telescope in Tasmania. Each search covered the spot for hours at a time. None of them found the signal, or anything like it.

In 2012 the direction was even reversed, with the reply shown above. If anyone is there to receive it, it will reach them in a very long time.

Today large surveys such as Breakthrough Listen scan huge parts of the sky for narrow signals like the Wow!, and machine learning helps sort through millions of candidates. They find plenty of signals. Almost all of them turn out to be satellites, aircraft or equipment on Earth.

=== The explanations | | Every serious idea so far, with the evidence on both sides and how it looks today.

A signal from another civilisation

The idea that made the signal famous, since 1977

For

It was very narrow, like a transmitter. It was close to the frequency of hydrogen, a frequency that astronomers have long argued aliens might choose because every scientist in the universe would know it. And it behaved exactly like a source fixed in the sky.

Against

It never repeated, which a beacon meant to be found surely would. It carried no message or pattern, and it lasted only 72 seconds. One single event, however strange, is very weak evidence for something as extraordinary as alien life.

No evidence
How likely, by today's research

Possible, but nothing supports it beyond the signal itself.

Interference from the Earth

The first thing every astronomer checks

For

Most unexpected radio signals are human-made: radar, aircraft, satellites, faulty electronics. A reflection from a passing satellite or piece of space debris could in principle produce a short, narrow spike.

Against

The 1420 MHz band is protected by international agreement, so nothing on Earth should transmit there. And the signal's 72-second rise and fall matched the movement of the sky, not that of something nearby. No satellite was found in the right place.

Unlikely
How likely, by today's research

Doesn't fit the shape or the frequency of the signal.

A passing comet

Proposed by Antonio Paris, 2017

For

Comets are surrounded by clouds of hydrogen, which give off radio waves at exactly this frequency. Two comets, undiscovered in 1977, were in that part of the sky around the time.

Against

Comets give off far too weak a signal to be seen so strongly. Other astronomers found that the comets were not in the right position, and the signal should then have appeared in both horns. Most scientists reject the idea.

Rejected by most
How likely, by today's research

An interesting idea that did not survive the checks.

A hydrogen cloud that suddenly flared

Proposed by Abel Méndez and colleagues, 2024

For

Searching old data from the Arecibo telescope, the team found weaker signals with the same narrow shape coming from cold clouds of hydrogen gas. A sudden burst of energy, for example from a magnetar, a very magnetic dead star, could make such a cloud flare brightly for a short time, at exactly the hydrogen frequency, and never in the same way again.

Against

It needs a rare and very bright event at the right moment and in the right place. The idea is new, has not yet been confirmed by other teams, and nobody has yet seen a flare as strong as the Wow! signal.

Most studied
How likely, by today's research

The leading natural explanation, still being tested.

Go deeper: why 1420 MHz is special

A hydrogen atom is one proton and one electron. Both of them spin, a little like tiny magnets. Very occasionally the electron flips its spin over, and the atom gives off a small burst of radio waves with a wavelength of 21 centimetres. That is a frequency of 1420.4 MHz.

Any single atom flips only about once in ten million years. But hydrogen is the most common element in the universe, and there is so much of it in space that the faint glow at 21 cm is everywhere. Astronomers use it to map the spiral arms of our galaxy, and that is why the band is protected: nobody on Earth is allowed to broadcast on it.

=== Other strange signals | | Space has surprised radio astronomers before. Most of the mysteries were solved, some in very unexpected ways.
1967

Little Green Men

The student Jocelyn Bell Burnell found a radio pulse repeating exactly every 1.34 seconds. It was so regular that her team half-jokingly labelled it LGM-1, for Little Green Men.

Solved: the first pulsar, a tiny spinning dead star.
1998 to 2015

The perytons

For years the Parkes telescope in Australia picked up short bursts that looked like they came from space, always during office hours.

Solved: the staff microwave oven, opened before it had finished.
2007

Fast radio bursts

A burst of radio waves lasting a few thousandths of a second, from far outside our galaxy. Thousands more have been found since.

Partly solved: many come from magnetars.
2019

BLC1

A narrow signal that seemed to come from Proxima Centauri, the nearest star to the Sun. For a few months it was the best candidate since the Wow!

Solved: interference from equipment on Earth.
=== What would solve it | | One more detection would change everything.

The trouble with the Wow! signal is that it happened once, was recorded by an old computer in a simple code, and was only noticed days later. There is no way to go back and measure it again. Every explanation has to fit six characters on a sheet of paper.

What would settle it is another signal like it, caught by modern equipment. Today's radio telescopes can record the full signal rather than a summary, point several dishes at the same spot at once, and react within seconds. If a similar burst appears from a hydrogen cloud, the flare explanation will be confirmed. If a narrow signal ever repeats from the same star, with a pattern in it, it would be one of the biggest discoveries in history.

Until then the Wow! signal stays what it has been since 1977: the most famous question mark in radio astronomy, and a reminder that the sky can still surprise us.
