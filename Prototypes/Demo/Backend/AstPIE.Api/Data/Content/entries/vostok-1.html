=== Choosing the first | 1959 to 1961 | Twenty young pilots, one seat, and a capsule that did not need a pilot at all.

In 1959 the Soviet Union began looking for its first cosmonauts. Doctors went through the records of thousands of military pilots and picked a few hundred for tests. In 1960 twenty were chosen. They had to be young, extremely healthy, and small: the capsule was tight, and every kilogram counted. Yuri Gagarin, a 26-year-old fighter pilot and the son of a carpenter, was 1.57 metres tall.

The training was hard. The candidates were spun in centrifuges, shut alone in silent chambers for days, and parachuted again and again. Out of the group, a smaller set of six trained for the first flight. By early April 1961 the choice came down to two: Gagarin, with Gherman Titov as his backup.

The Vostok spacecraft was designed to fly itself. Nobody knew for sure whether a person could think clearly, or even swallow, in weightlessness, so the controls were locked. A sealed envelope on board held the code to unlock them in an emergency. Gagarin was told the code before launch anyway.

=== The spacecraft | 1960 to 1961 | A ball for the pilot, a cone of equipment behind it, and seven test flights first.

Vostok had two parts. The pilot rode in a sphere 2.3 metres across, covered in a heat shield, with three small windows. Behind it was an instrument section shaped like two cones joined together, with the batteries, the oxygen and the retro-rocket that would bring the craft home. In all it weighed about 4.7 tonnes.

A sphere was chosen because it is stable as it falls through the air, whichever way it is turned. That made it simple, but it also meant the capsule could not glide or steer as it came down. It would land hard, so the pilot would not stay inside: at about seven kilometres up he would be thrown out in his seat and come down on his own parachute.

Before any person flew, the design was tested on unmanned flights carrying dogs and a life-size mannequin nicknamed Ivan Ivanovich. Some of those tests failed. Two dogs, Pchyolka and Mushka, died when their capsule was destroyed in December 1960. The last two tests, in March 1961, both worked.

=== Let's go | 12 April 1961, 09:07 Moscow time | One word on the radio, and a rocket rising from the steppe.

Early on 12 April 1961, Gagarin and Titov were driven to the launch pad at Tyuratam, the same pad that had launched Sputnik. Gagarin rode the lift to the top and was strapped into the capsule. There was a long wait while a hatch seal was checked and refitted. Over the radio, Sergei Korolev kept talking to him calmly, and Gagarin asked for music.

At 09:07 Moscow time the engines ignited. As the rocket lifted off, Gagarin called out a single word: Poyekhali!, "Let's go!". The rocket pressed him into his seat with several times his normal weight. After about two minutes the four side boosters fell away; after five minutes the core stage shut down, and the upper stage carried him the rest of the way to orbit.

=== One trip around the world | 12 April 1961 | 108 minutes from launch to landing, most of them in weightlessness.

Vostok 1 went around the Earth once, at heights between about 170 and 330 kilometres, moving at more than 27,000 kilometres per hour. Gagarin reported what he saw: the curve of the horizon, the blue layer of the atmosphere, the darkness of space. He ate and drank a little, wrote notes, and found that he could work normally while floating. When his pencil drifted away, he kept reporting by radio.

Over Africa, the retro-rocket fired to slow the craft down. Then something went wrong. The instrument section was supposed to separate from the sphere. It stayed attached by a bundle of cables, and the two parts tumbled together for about ten minutes until the cables burned through in the heat of re-entry. Gagarin felt the spinning and the rising heat, and kept calm.

=== Down in a field | 12 April 1961 | A man in an orange suit, and a farmer and her granddaughter.

At about seven kilometres above the ground, Gagarin was ejected from the capsule, as planned, and came down under his own parachute. He landed in a field near the city of Engels, on the Volga, not far from where the capsule hit the ground.

The first people to see him were a farmer, Anna Takhtarova, and her granddaughter. A man in a bright orange suit and a white helmet was walking towards them across the field. Gagarin told them not to be afraid: he was a Soviet citizen, and he needed a telephone to call Moscow.

For years the Soviet Union said that he had landed inside his capsule. The international rules for a record flight said the pilot had to land with his craft, and the parachute landing was only admitted later.

=== The world's first spaceman | April 1961 onwards | Overnight, a young pilot became the most famous person on Earth.

The flight was announced while Gagarin was still in orbit. Two days later he was welcomed in Moscow by huge crowds in Red Square. Over the next months he travelled around the world, from Britain to Brazil to Japan, and everywhere people lined the streets to see him.

In America the news came as a second shock after Sputnik. Three weeks later, on 5 May 1961, Alan Shepard became the first American in space, on a fifteen-minute flight that went up and came straight back down. On 25 May, President John F. Kennedy told Congress that America should land a man on the Moon before the end of the decade.

Gagarin never flew in space again; he was too valuable to risk. He went back to flight training, and on 27 March 1968 he died, aged 34, when his jet trainer crashed during a training flight. Every year on 12 April, people around the world celebrate the anniversary of his flight.
