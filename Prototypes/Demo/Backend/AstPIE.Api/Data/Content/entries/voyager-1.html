=== A once-in-175-years chance | 1960s to 1972 | The outer planets were lining up, and a clever trick could visit them all.

In the 1960s, engineers at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory noticed something rare. In the late 1970s, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune would all be on the same side of the Sun, in an arc. A spacecraft launched at the right moment could fly past one planet and use its gravity to swing on to the next. That alignment only comes around about once every 175 years.

The trick is called a gravity assist. As a spacecraft falls towards a moving planet and swings around it, it can steal a little of the planet's speed around the Sun. Without it, reaching Neptune would take thirty years. With it, about twelve.

A grand plan to visit all four planets was too expensive, so NASA approved a smaller one in 1972: two spacecraft, built to visit Jupiter and Saturn. They were called Voyager 1 and Voyager 2. Quietly, the engineers built them to last much longer than four years.

=== The spacecraft | 1972 to 1977 | A big dish, a long boom, and nuclear batteries instead of solar panels.

Each Voyager is about the size of a small car, with a mass of about 825 kilograms. The most obvious part is a white dish antenna 3.7 metres across, which always points back at the Earth. Below it sits a ten-sided box holding the computers, the radio and the tanks of fuel for small thrusters.

Solar panels would be useless so far from the Sun, where sunlight is hundreds of times weaker. Instead, each Voyager carries three nuclear batteries, called radioisotope thermoelectric generators. They turn the heat of decaying plutonium into electricity. At launch they produced about 470 watts. They lose a few watts every year, and the team has been switching instruments off one by one to save power.

On long booms the spacecraft carries cameras, a magnetometer and instruments to measure particles, radio waves and light. Its computers have less memory than a simple modern calculator. They are still running.

=== A message in a bottle | 1977 | A gold-plated record for anyone who might find it.

Both Voyagers will leave the Solar System, so NASA asked a committee led by the astronomer Carl Sagan to choose a message for anyone who might one day find one. They made a gold-plated copper record, like an old vinyl disc, with a needle to play it and instructions engraved on the cover.

The record holds greetings in 55 languages, sounds of the Earth such as rain, birds, a heartbeat and a kiss, music from many cultures, from Bach to Chuck Berry, and 116 pictures encoded as sound. The cover shows, in symbols, how to play it and where the Earth is, using the positions of pulsars as a map.

Nobody expects it to be found. Voyager 1 will not come close to another star for about 40,000 years. But it is a picture of humanity in 1977, sent out on purpose.

=== Launch | 5 September 1977 | Voyager 1 left second, on a faster path, and overtook its twin.

Voyager 2 was launched first, on 20 August 1977. Voyager 1 followed on 5 September 1977, on a Titan IIIE rocket with a Centaur upper stage from Cape Canaveral. It was named Voyager 1 because it was on a faster, shorter path, and would reach Jupiter first. By December 1977 it had overtaken its twin.

On the way, on 18 September 1977, it turned its camera back and took the first picture ever of the Earth and the Moon together in a single frame, two crescents hanging in the dark.

=== Jupiter | March 1979 | Volcanoes on another world, and a ring nobody had seen.

Voyager 1 passed closest to Jupiter on 5 March 1979. Its pictures showed the planet's storms in astonishing detail, including the Great Red Spot, a storm bigger than the Earth that has been raging for centuries.

The biggest surprise came a few days later. On 9 March, the navigation engineer Linda Morabito was studying a picture of the moon Io and noticed a strange bright crescent at its edge. It was a plume of gas and dust from a volcano, rising hundreds of kilometres into space. Io turned out to be the most volcanic place in the Solar System, heated from inside by the pull of Jupiter's gravity. It was the first time active volcanoes had been seen anywhere except on the Earth.

Voyager 1 also found a thin, faint ring around Jupiter, and new small moons. Then it used Jupiter's gravity to swing on towards Saturn.

=== Saturn and Titan | November 1980 | A choice that ended its tour of the planets.

Voyager 1 reached Saturn in November 1980. It showed that the rings are made of thousands of narrow ringlets, with strange dark "spokes" that come and go, and that some thin rings are kept in shape by tiny "shepherd" moons on either side.

The scientists also wanted a close look at Titan, Saturn's largest moon and the only moon with a thick atmosphere. To get close, Voyager 1 had to pass behind Titan, and that path would throw it upwards out of the plane of the planets, so it could never reach Uranus or Neptune. They chose Titan. On 12 November 1980 Voyager 1 flew past it and found an atmosphere thicker than the Earth's, mostly nitrogen, wrapped in an orange haze that hid the surface completely.

Voyager 2 carried on to Uranus in 1986 and Neptune in 1989, and is still the only spacecraft to have visited them. Voyager 1 headed out of the Solar System.

=== A pale blue dot | 14 February 1990 | One last look back, from six billion kilometres away.

Carl Sagan had long argued for one more picture: Voyager 1 should turn around and photograph the planets from the outside, a family portrait of the Solar System. On 14 February 1990, about six billion kilometres from the Sun, it took sixty pictures. Then its cameras were switched off for good to save power.

In one of the frames the Earth is a single tiny dot, less than a pixel, caught in a beam of scattered sunlight. Sagan called it the "pale blue dot", and wrote that every person who has ever lived, lived there, on a mote of dust suspended in a sunbeam.

=== Into interstellar space | 1998 to today | The farthest object ever made by people, still sending data home.

In February 1998 Voyager 1 passed the earlier probe Pioneer 10 and became the most distant human-made object. In December 2004 it crossed the termination shock, where the wind from the Sun suddenly slows down. And on 25 August 2012, about 18 billion kilometres from the Sun, it left the bubble of the solar wind altogether, the heliosphere, and entered interstellar space, the first spacecraft ever to do so.

It still talks to the Earth through the large dishes of NASA's Deep Space Network. Its radio is about as powerful as a refrigerator light, and by the time the signal arrives it is billions of times weaker than the power of a watch battery. In late 2026 Voyager 1 is close to one light-day from the Earth: a command sent to it takes almost a full day to arrive.

In November 2023 it suddenly started sending nonsense. The team traced the problem to a single broken memory chip in one of its computers and, in April 2024, moved the affected code to another part of the memory, by radio, from 24 billion kilometres away. It worked. The Voyager team expects to keep at least one instrument running into the 2030s, until the power finally runs out. After that, Voyager 1 will keep drifting through the galaxy, with no force left to stop it.
