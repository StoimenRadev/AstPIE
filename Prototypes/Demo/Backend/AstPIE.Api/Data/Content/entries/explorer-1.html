=== The answer to Sputnik | October to December 1957 | America had a satellite programme. It just was not ready, and the world was watching.

When Sputnik 1 began beeping over America in October 1957, the United States already had a satellite on the way. It was called Vanguard, and it was run by the Navy as a showcase of peaceful science for the International Geophysical Year. It was also late, and its rocket had never flown in full.

A second team had been waiting for this moment. At the Army Ballistic Missile Agency in Huntsville, Alabama, the rocket engineer Wernher von Braun and his group had a proven missile, the Redstone, and a plan to turn it into a satellite launcher. In 1956 they had flown a version of it so high that, with one more stage, it could have reached orbit. They had been told not to try: Vanguard was the official project.

After Sputnik, and then the much larger Sputnik 2 with the dog Laika on board, the pressure became enormous. On 8 November 1957 the Army was given permission to try. On 6 December the Navy went first. On live television, the Vanguard rocket rose about a metre, lost power and fell back in a ball of fire. Newspapers called it "Flopnik" and "Kaputnik". The Army team had 84 days to do better.

=== Three teams, one satellite | November 1957 to January 1958 | A rocket from Huntsville, a satellite from Pasadena, an instrument from Iowa.

Explorer 1 was built by three groups working at the same time. Von Braun's team in Huntsville prepared the rocket. The Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Pasadena, California, led by the New Zealand-born engineer William Pickering, built the satellite and the upper stages that would push it into orbit. At the University of Iowa, the physicist James Van Allen prepared the science instrument.

The satellite was a long, thin tube about two metres long and only 15 centimetres wide, attached to the top of the last rocket stage. It weighed about 14 kilograms in all, including that empty stage, and carried a little more than 8 kilograms of instruments: thermometers, a microphone to detect hits from dust grains, two radio transmitters and, most importantly, a Geiger counter to count cosmic rays.

The rocket was a stretched Redstone with three small upper stages stacked on top. Those stages were clusters of little solid rockets, eleven in the second stage, three in the third and one in the fourth, spun up like a top so that they would fly straight. The whole stack was called Juno I.

=== Launch night | 31 January 1958 | Cape Canaveral, a little before eleven at night, after days of bad weather.

Strong winds high above Florida had already delayed the launch several times. On the evening of 31 January 1958 they finally dropped. At 22:48 local time the Juno I lifted off from Cape Canaveral. The Redstone burned for about two and a half minutes, then the spinning upper stages fired one after another and pushed the satellite sideways at orbital speed.

Then came the wait. Nobody could be sure the satellite was in orbit until a tracking station heard it come back around. The signal was expected at a station in California after about 106 minutes. The minutes went by and nothing came. Explorer 1 had been pushed into a higher orbit than planned, so it took longer to go around. When the signal finally arrived, several minutes late, the room where the teams were waiting broke into cheers.

At about half past one in the morning, Pickering, Van Allen and von Braun walked into a press conference in Washington and lifted a full-size model of the satellite above their heads. The photograph became one of the most famous images of the early space age.

=== A counter that went silent | February to May 1958 | The Geiger counter stopped counting. That was the discovery.

Van Allen's Geiger counter was meant to count cosmic rays, fast particles that arrive from deep space. At low altitude it worked as expected, counting about thirty particles every second. But when Explorer 1 climbed high on its orbit, the readings did something strange: they dropped to zero.

Zero made no sense. There should have been more cosmic rays higher up, not fewer. Van Allen and his students worked out the answer. The counter was not seeing too little radiation. It was seeing far too much. The particles were arriving so quickly that the counter was overwhelmed and could not count them separately.

The next satellite, Explorer 3, carried a small tape recorder so that it could store a whole orbit of readings and play them back. It confirmed the idea. In May 1958 Van Allen announced that the Earth is surrounded by zones of fast, charged particles trapped by its magnetic field. They are called the Van Allen belts, and they were the first great scientific discovery of the space age.

Go deeper: what are the belts made of?

The Earth's magnetic field acts like an invisible bottle. Electrons and protons get caught in it and spiral back and forth along the field lines between north and south. There are two main belts: an inner one, from about 1,000 to 6,000 kilometres up, full of energetic protons, and an outer one, much further out, mostly of electrons.

The belts matter for every spacecraft. The radiation in them damages electronics and is dangerous for people, which is why the Apollo crews were sent through them quickly, and why satellites that stay in them need extra shielding.

=== The spinning surprise | February 1958 | A satellite designed to spin like a bullet began to tumble. Engineers learned why.

Explorer 1 was meant to spin around its long axis, like a rifle bullet, to keep it steady. Within its first orbit it started to wobble, and soon it was tumbling end over end, turning around its short axis instead.

The cause was its four whip antennas. They bent a little as the satellite spun, and each bend turned a tiny amount of the spin energy into heat. A long, thin object that slowly loses energy this way always ends up spinning around the axis where its mass is spread out the most, which for a tube is the short axis.

The lesson was learned at once. Later spinning satellites were built short and wide, like a drum, so that the stable way to spin is also the one the designers want. It is one of the first examples of space engineers learning a rule of physics from a satellite already in orbit.

=== A long quiet orbit | 1958 to 1970 | The radio stopped after four months. The satellite stayed up for twelve years.

Explorer 1 had no solar panels. Its batteries kept the main transmitter running until 23 May 1958, almost four months after launch. After that the satellite was silent, but it was far from finished.

Its orbit took it as low as about 360 kilometres and as high as about 2,550 kilometres, much higher than Sputnik's. Up there the air is so thin that it slows a satellite only very gently. Explorer 1 went round the Earth more than 58,000 times. It finally fell back into the atmosphere and burned up over the Pacific on 31 March 1970, twelve years after launch.

=== What it changed | 1958 | America was in the race, and in July 1958 it got a space agency.

Explorer 1 did more than catch up. Sputnik had proven that a satellite could be launched. Explorer 1 proved that a satellite could do real science, and it made a discovery nobody had predicted.

It also changed how America organised its space work. The rivalry between the Army and the Navy had cost months. In July 1958 Congress created a single civilian agency to run the programme, the National Aeronautics and Space Administration. NASA opened on 1 October 1958, and the Jet Propulsion Laboratory and, later, von Braun's team in Huntsville became part of it.

Explorer became the name of a long family of science satellites. More than ninety have flown since, studying the Sun, the stars and the space around the Earth.
