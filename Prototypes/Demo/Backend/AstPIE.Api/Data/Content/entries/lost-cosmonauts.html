=== The rumours | Late 1950s to 1960s | A secret space programme, and stories that filled the silence.

The Soviet space programme was run in deep secrecy. Launches were announced only after they had succeeded. Failures were never mentioned. The names of the engineers were hidden, and even the man in charge, Sergei Korolev, was known to the public only as "the Chief Designer".

Where there is silence, stories grow. From the late 1950s, newspapers in the West began to print reports that the Soviet Union had already sent people into space and lost them, and covered it up. Some reports gave names, dates and details. None of them came with evidence that could be checked.

The idea was believable for a simple reason: the secrecy was real. If a cosmonaut had died, the world would not have been told. So the question was not whether the Soviet Union would have hidden a death, but whether one had happened.

=== The brothers of Torre Bert | 1957 to 1960s | Two young radio amateurs near Turin, with home-made aerials and tape recorders.

When Sputnik 1 was launched in 1957, two brothers in Turin, Italy, Achille and Gian Battista Judica-Cordiglia, were among the many radio amateurs who tuned in to its beeps. They were good at it. They built a listening station in an old German bunker on a hill outside the city, which they called Torre Bert, and filled it with aerials and recording equipment.

Over the next few years they recorded signals from many satellites, and their station became well known. Journalists visited them, and their recordings were played on radio and television in Italy and abroad.

=== What they said they heard | 1960 and 1961 | An SOS fading into space, a beating heart, a woman's voice in flames.

The brothers' most famous claims went far beyond satellite beeps. They said they had recorded secret Soviet flights that had gone wrong.

In November 1960, they said, they picked up a Morse code SOS signal moving away from the Earth, as if a spacecraft had gone the wrong way and was lost in space. In early 1961 they released a recording of heavy breathing and what they said was a failing heartbeat. And in May 1961 came the most dramatic of all: a woman's voice, speaking in Russian, saying that she was getting hot and could see flames, as if a capsule was burning up on its way back to Earth.

The recordings were powerful to listen to, and for many people they seemed to prove the rumours. If they were real, several cosmonauts had died before and after Gagarin, and the world had been lied to.

=== The recordings, checked | 1960s to 1990s | The tapes do not fit what we know about the flights.

Over the years, engineers, historians and Russian speakers examined the claims. The problems piled up.

The frequencies the brothers said they used do not match those Soviet spacecraft are known to have used for voice. The orbits and timings of the claimed flights do not match any rocket launch tracked by Western radar, which watched Soviet launch sites closely. And Russian speakers who listened to the "woman cosmonaut" recording found that her speech made little sense, with mistakes no Soviet pilot reading from a checklist would make.

The heartbeat and breathing are not evidence of anything on their own: the Soviet Union did send dogs and dummies into orbit, and their instruments sent back exactly that kind of medical data.

Go deeper: why a heartbeat on the radio is not surprising

The test flights before Gagarin carried dogs fitted with sensors, and a life-size mannequin. The Soviet ground stations wanted to know how a living body coped with launch and weightlessness, so the spacecraft sent back heart and breathing signals by radio. Picking up such signals proves only that a spacecraft was transmitting medical data, not that a person was on board.

The secrecy was real, and it fed the legend.

=== A real hidden death | 23 March 1961 | A fire in a training chamber, and a secret kept for 25 years.

There was one real death, and it was hidden. Valentin Bondarenko, at 24 the youngest of the first group of cosmonauts, was near the end of a ten-day test in a sealed pressure chamber in Moscow. The chamber was filled with air rich in oxygen, at low pressure, to copy the conditions inside a spacecraft.

When he took off his medical sensors, he wiped his skin with a pad of cotton soaked in alcohol and tossed it away. It landed on a hot electric plate. In the oxygen-rich air it burst into flames, and the fire spread to his woollen training suit. It took the staff several minutes to open the chamber, because the pressure had to be equalised first. Bondarenko died in hospital about eight hours later. It was 23 March 1961, less than three weeks before Gagarin's flight.

The death was kept secret. It was only revealed in 1986, in an article by the journalist Yaroslav Golovanov in the newspaper Izvestia. Six years after Bondarenko, a similar fire in pure oxygen killed the three astronauts of Apollo 1 in America.

=== Faces removed from photographs | 1961 to 1963 | Cosmonauts who left the programme also vanished from its pictures.

Another real secret made the rumours look even more convincing. Several of the first cosmonauts left the programme early, some for medical reasons and some for discipline. The best known is Grigori Nelyubov, who had been one of the six candidates for the first flight. In 1963 he was dismissed after an argument with a military patrol.

When official photographs of the cosmonaut group were later published, he had been carefully painted out of them. Years later, people comparing the original and the published photos spotted the differences: a missing man in a row, a shadow with nobody casting it. To many, that looked like proof that cosmonauts were disappearing. In fact the people removed were alive, and had simply left the programme.

=== What the archives showed | After 1991 | When the Soviet Union ended, the files were opened.

After the Soviet Union broke up in 1991, historians from Russia and abroad were able to read documents that had been secret for decades, and the engineers and cosmonauts of the early years were able to speak freely. Many wrote memoirs describing failures, accidents and near-disasters in great detail.

They described Bondarenko's death, the removed photographs, the explosions of rockets on the pad and the deaths of crews that were already publicly known, such as Vladimir Komarov in 1967 and the three cosmonauts of Soyuz 11 in 1971. They did not describe any secret flight that had killed a cosmonaut in space. The American space historian James Oberg, who spent years investigating Soviet cover-ups, came to the same conclusion.

=== The verdict | Today | Debunked, but the questions made sense.

The case of the lost cosmonauts is closed: nobody was secretly lost in space. The famous recordings do not match any real flight, and every early cosmonaut has been accounted for.

But the rumours were not foolish. They grew out of real secrecy, a real hidden death and real faces removed from photographs. When information is hidden, people fill the gaps with stories, and some of those stories are hard to kill. The lesson of the case is not only about space. It is about why openness matters.
