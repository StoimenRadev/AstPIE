=== The race | | Two countries promised the world a satellite. Only one of them was keeping it simple.

In the 1950s scientists around the world were planning something enormous: the International Geophysical Year, eighteen months from July 1957 to December 1958 in which sixty-seven countries would study the Earth together, from the ocean floor to the edge of space. In 1955 both the United States and the Soviet Union announced that, as part of it, they would launch the first artificial satellite.

Few people in the West took the Soviet promise seriously. The American plan, called Vanguard, was public, well funded and run by civilian scientists. The Soviet plan was secret, and it was led by a man whose name almost nobody outside the Soviet Union would learn for another decade: Sergei Korolev, the Chief Designer.

Why "Sputnik"?

The word means "travelling companion" in Russian, and in astronomy it is used for any moon or satellite.

Korolev's real job was building weapons. His team had designed the R-7, the world's first intercontinental ballistic missile, a rocket powerful enough to carry a nuclear warhead to the other side of the planet. But Korolev and his colleague Mikhail Tikhonravov had dreamed of space flight since the 1930s, and they saw that the R-7 could do something else. If it could throw a warhead halfway around the world, it could throw a small satellite fast enough to go all the way around, and keep going.

The Soviet satellite that had been officially approved was a large scientific laboratory known as Object D, packed with instruments. By late 1956 it was clear it would not be ready in time. Its instruments were late, it was too heavy, and the first test flights of the R-7 were failing. Korolev feared the Americans would get there first. In January 1957 he proposed a shortcut: a tiny satellite that could be built in weeks, with no scientific instruments at all, only a radio transmitter. It was called PS-1, from the Russian for "the simplest satellite". The government agreed.

Then the R-7 had to work. Its first three launches, in the spring and summer of 1957, failed. The fourth, on 21 August, flew across the whole Soviet Union and dropped its dummy warhead on target in Kamchatka. A second success followed in September. The rocket was ready. The race, in Korolev's mind, was about to be won.

=== Building the ball | | The simplest satellite had to work perfectly, first time, with nobody able to fix it.

PS-1 was a sphere 58 centimetres across, about the size of a car tyre or a very large beach ball. Its shell was made of two halves of a light aluminium alloy, each only 2 millimetres thick, joined around the middle with 36 bolts. The surface was polished until it shone like a mirror. That was not for show: a shiny ball reflects most of the sunlight that falls on it, which helped stop the inside from overheating, and it also made the ball easier to track from the ground.

Why a sphere? Because a ball has no front and no back. Nobody would be able to control which way the satellite pointed once it was in orbit, so its antennas had to send a signal in every direction at once. Four antennas, two of them 2.4 metres long and two 2.9 metres long, were fixed near the top of the ball and swept backwards like whiskers. When the satellite separated from its rocket, springs swung them out into position.

Inside, most of the space and most of the weight was taken by batteries: three silver-zinc batteries weighing about 51 kilograms out of the satellite's total of 83.6. They powered a radio transmitter with an output of just one watt, about as much as a small torch bulb, and a fan that circulated nitrogen gas inside the sealed ball to keep the electronics at a steady temperature.

The designers added one clever trick. The beep was not quite the same all the time. If the temperature or the pressure inside the ball went outside safe limits, the length of the pulses would change. So even the simplest satellite was sending a little information home: by listening carefully, engineers on the ground could tell whether its insides were still healthy, and whether a meteoroid had punctured the shell.

=== Launch night | | 4 October 1957, a launch pad in the empty steppe of Kazakhstan.

The launch site was a secret test range near the railway stop of Tyuratam, in the dry steppe of Soviet Kazakhstan. The world would later know it as the Baikonur Cosmodrome. On the evening of 4 October 1957 the R-7 stood on its pad with PS-1 tucked inside a cone on top. At 22:28 Moscow time its engines ignited.

The R-7 was an unusual rocket. Instead of stacking stages on top of each other, it had a central core surrounded by four boosters, like the petals of a flower. All five lit on the ground at once. About two minutes into the flight the four boosters burned out and fell away, and the core kept firing alone. Close to five minutes after lift-off it shut down, and the satellite separated from it at a speed of about 8 kilometres per second.

It was not a perfect flight. One of the engines was slow to reach full power, and the core stage shut down a little early. Sputnik ended up in a lower orbit than planned: its highest point was 939 kilometres instead of more than 1 400. It did not matter. The ball was in orbit, and about an hour and a half later, when it came around the planet for the first time and passed back over the launch site, the team heard its signal. Only then did they celebrate.

Only when the beep came back, on its first full trip around the Earth, did the team believe it had really worked.

The next morning, the official newspaper Pravda reported the launch in a short, dry article, not even at the top of its front page. The Soviet leadership had not yet realised what they had done. Within days, they would.

Go deeper: why the orbit is an oval

A satellite released exactly horizontally at exactly the right speed would follow a perfect circle. Real rockets are never quite that precise. Sputnik was released at its lowest point, about 215 kilometres up, a little faster than a circular orbit needs. That extra speed carried it higher on the far side of the Earth, up to 939 kilometres, before gravity brought it back down to 215 kilometres again. The result is an ellipse, an oval with the centre of the Earth at one of its two focus points.

Johannes Kepler worked out in 1609 that planets move on ellipses around the Sun. Newton showed why: it is what the law of gravity produces for anything that is moving too fast for a circle but not fast enough to escape.

=== The beep | | For three weeks, anyone with a shortwave radio could hear the space age begin.

Sputnik sent its signal on two frequencies, 20.005 and 40.002 megahertz. These were chosen on purpose: they were in bands that ordinary radio amateurs could receive with the equipment they already had at home. The signal was a simple pulse, beep, pause, beep, each lasting about three tenths of a second, the two frequencies taking turns.

Within hours, radio stations and amateurs on every continent were picking it up. The American network NBC recorded it and played it on the air, telling listeners that this sound separated the old world from the new. People stood in their gardens at dawn and dusk to watch for a moving point of light, though what most of them saw was not the tiny ball itself but the much larger empty core of the rocket, which followed it around the Earth.

[[radio]]

The signal was more useful than it sounded. At the Applied Physics Laboratory of Johns Hopkins University in the USA, two young physicists, William Guier and George Weiffenbach, noticed that the pitch of the beep rose as Sputnik approached and fell as it moved away, the same effect that makes a passing ambulance siren change its note. By measuring this shift carefully they could work out the satellite's entire orbit from a single receiving station.

Their colleagues then turned the idea around. If you know exactly where a satellite is, you can use the same shift to work out exactly where you are. That idea became Transit, the first satellite navigation system, used by the US Navy from the 1960s, and the ancestor of the GPS in every phone today.

Go deeper: why two frequencies?

High above the Earth is the ionosphere, a layer of air charged by sunlight. It bends and slows radio waves, and it bends lower frequencies more than higher ones. By comparing how the 20 MHz and 40 MHz signals arrived at the same moment, scientists could measure how many charged particles the signal had passed through. Sputnik was the first object to probe the ionosphere from above.

=== The world reacts | | A ball the size of a beach ball changed politics, schools and science on the other side of the planet.

In the Soviet Union the launch became a matter of enormous pride, and the leader Nikita Khrushchev quickly understood its value as propaganda. In the United States, the reaction was closer to shock. Americans had assumed they led the world in technology. Now a Soviet satellite was passing over their heads several times a day, and everyone understood what that meant: a rocket that could put a ball into orbit could also put a warhead anywhere on Earth.

The newspapers called it the "Sputnik crisis". Things got worse a month later, on 3 November, when Sputnik 2 carried a dog named Laika into orbit, the first living animal to circle the Earth. Then, on 6 December 1957, live on television, the first American attempt to launch a satellite with a Vanguard rocket rose about a metre off the pad, lost power and exploded. The press nicknamed it "Flopnik".

The United States recovered quickly. On 31 January 1958 a team led by Wernher von Braun launched Explorer 1, which went on to discover the belts of radiation that surround the Earth. In July 1958 the US Congress created a new civilian space agency, NASA, which opened on 1 October that year. A new military agency for advanced research, now called DARPA, had been set up in February. And in September the National Defense Education Act poured money into teaching science, mathematics and languages in American schools and universities.

In other words, one small satellite started a competition between two superpowers that would last for twenty years, from the first satellite to the first person in space and on to the Moon. Historians call it the space race, and Sputnik fired the starting gun.

=== Silence and fall | | The batteries ran out first. The air finished the job.

On 26 October 1957, after twenty-two days, the batteries ran out and the beep stopped. Sputnik itself did not stop. It kept circling the Earth in silence, as the first law of motion says it should, because there was almost nothing in space to slow it down.

Almost nothing. Even at 215 kilometres there is still a tiny amount of air. Every time Sputnik swept through the lowest point of its orbit, that thin air took away a little of its speed. With slightly less speed, it could not climb quite as high on the other side of the planet, so the high point of its orbit came down first, and the oval slowly became rounder and lower. Scientists who tracked this shrinking learned for the first time how dense the air really is high above the Earth.

By the start of January 1958 the whole orbit was low enough for the air to win. On 4 January, after about 1 440 trips around the Earth and some 70 million kilometres, Sputnik 1 plunged into the thicker atmosphere and burned up. Its rocket stage had already fallen a month earlier. Nothing of either survived.

=== What it changed | | Every satellite in the sky today has Sputnik as its great-grandparent.

Sputnik carried no camera, no scientific instruments and no passengers. It did one thing: it proved that people could build something that would stay in space. Everything followed from that. Within four years the same family of rockets had carried Yuri Gagarin around the Earth. Within twelve, astronauts were walking on the Moon.

Today more than ten thousand working satellites circle the Earth. They forecast the weather, carry television and internet signals, watch forests and ice caps, help farmers and ships, and tell your phone where you are. The pad Sputnik left from, later named Gagarin's Start, sent crews into space for more than sixty years. And the Soyuz rockets that still fly from Baikonur are direct descendants of the R-7 that lifted a polished metal ball into orbit on an October night in 1957.

=== The physics | | Why didn't Sputnik fall straight down? It did. It just kept missing.

Imagine standing on a very tall mountain and throwing a ball horizontally. It curves down and lands some distance away. Throw it faster and it lands further away. Newton imagined throwing it so fast that, as it fell, the curved surface of the Earth dropped away beneath it just as quickly. The ball would keep falling, and keep missing the ground, all the way around the planet. That is an orbit.

Two laws work together here. Newton's first law says the satellite wants to keep moving in a straight line at the same speed. Gravity pulls it towards the centre of the Earth all the time. The result is neither a straight line nor a fall to the ground, but a curve that wraps around the planet. Without gravity Sputnik would have flown off into space in a straight line. Without its speed, it would have fallen straight down.

Go deeper: how fast is fast enough?

For a round orbit, gravity must provide exactly the pull needed to bend the path into a circle. Setting the two equal gives the orbital speed:

v = √(GM ÷ r) = √(398 600 ÷ 6 586) ≈ 7.8 km/s

Here GM is the Earth's mass multiplied by the gravitational constant, 398 600 km³/s², and r is the distance from the centre of the Earth: 6 371 km of planet plus 215 km of height. That speed is about 28 000 km/h, enough to cross the Atlantic Ocean in a quarter of an hour.
