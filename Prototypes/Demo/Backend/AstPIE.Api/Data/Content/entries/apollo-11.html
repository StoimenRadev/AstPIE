=== A promise | 1961 to 1968 | A president set a deadline. It took 400,000 people to keep it.

On 25 May 1961, six weeks after Yuri Gagarin's flight, President John F. Kennedy stood before the American Congress and set a goal: to land a man on the Moon and return him safely to the Earth before the decade was out. At that moment the United States had sent one astronaut into space, for fifteen minutes.

What followed was one of the largest projects in history. About 400,000 people worked on Apollo, in NASA centres and in thousands of companies. Two earlier programmes prepared the way: Mercury, which put single astronauts into orbit, and Gemini, which taught crews to stay in space for two weeks, to work outside their spacecraft and to meet and dock with another craft in orbit. All of that would be needed for the Moon.

The programme also had its tragedy. In January 1967 a fire broke out inside the Apollo 1 capsule during a ground test, and the three astronauts on board, Gus Grissom, Ed White and Roger Chaffee, were killed. The spacecraft was redesigned. Less than two years later, in December 1968, Apollo 8 carried three men around the Moon and back.

=== The crew and the machines | 1969 | Three astronauts, a giant rocket, and two spacecraft called Columbia and Eagle.

The crew of Apollo 11 were all experienced test pilots who had flown in space before. Neil Armstrong was the commander. Buzz Aldrin would fly the lunar module down to the surface with him. Michael Collins would stay in orbit around the Moon, in the command module, and wait for them.

They would travel in two spacecraft joined together. The command module Columbia, a cone about 3.9 metres wide, carried them to the Moon and back and was the only part that would return to Earth. The lunar module Eagle, a spidery craft with four legs that could only fly in space, would carry two of them down to the surface and back up again.

Everything was launched by the Saturn V, the most powerful rocket that had ever flown. It stood 110 metres tall and weighed almost 3,000 tonnes when full. Its first stage burned about 13 tonnes of fuel every second.

=== Launch and the long coast | 16 to 19 July 1969 | Three days of silence across 384,000 kilometres of space.

Apollo 11 lifted off from the Kennedy Space Center in Florida at 09:32 local time on 16 July 1969. About a million people watched from the beaches and roads around the Cape. Twelve minutes later the crew was in orbit around the Earth.

After one and a half orbits the third stage fired again and sent them towards the Moon. Collins then turned Columbia around, docked with Eagle, which was stored inside the top of the rocket, and pulled it free. For three days the joined spacecraft coasted across space, slowing down as the Earth's gravity pulled back on them, then speeding up again as the Moon's gravity took over. On 19 July they fired their engine behind the Moon and went into orbit around it.

=== The landing | 20 July 1969 | Alarms, a field of boulders, and less than a minute of fuel.

On 20 July Armstrong and Aldrin climbed into Eagle, undocked from Columbia and began to descend. A few minutes into the descent, the computer started flashing alarms that neither of them had seen before, "1202" and "1201". It was overloaded with data. In Houston a young engineer, Steve Bales, judged that the computer was still doing the important jobs, and the call came back: go.

Then Armstrong looked out of the window. The computer was taking them straight into a crater surrounded by boulders the size of cars. He took over the controls and flew Eagle on, skimming over the rocks, looking for a flat place. Fuel was running out. Aldrin called out the height and speed. In Houston, the warnings counted down: sixty seconds, thirty seconds.

At 20:17 universal time, a probe under one of the legs touched the ground. Armstrong's voice came over the radio.

Houston, Tranquility Base here. The Eagle has landed.
Neil Armstrong, 20 July 1969
=== One small step | 20 and 21 July 1969 | Two and a half hours on the surface, watched by hundreds of millions of people.

About six and a half hours after landing, Armstrong climbed down the ladder. A television camera on the side of Eagle sent pictures back to the Earth. At 02:56 universal time on 21 July, he put his left boot onto the Moon.

That's one small step for man, one giant leap for mankind.
Neil Armstrong, 21 July 1969

Aldrin followed twenty minutes later and described what he saw as "magnificent desolation". The two men walked, ran and hopped in the low gravity, where they weighed a sixth of what they weighed on Earth. They collected rocks and soil, planted an American flag, unveiled a plaque on the leg of Eagle, and spoke by radio with President Richard Nixon.

They also set up experiments. A seismometer listened for moonquakes. A sheet of aluminium foil caught particles of the solar wind. And a small panel of mirrors, a laser reflector, was placed facing the Earth. Observatories still bounce laser beams off it today to measure the distance to the Moon to within a few centimetres.

After about two and a half hours outside they climbed back in. Their boot prints are still there: with no wind or rain on the Moon, they could last for millions of years.

=== Alone behind the Moon | 20 and 21 July 1969 | While two men walked on the Moon, a third flew around it by himself.

Michael Collins stayed in Columbia, going around the Moon every two hours. Each time he passed behind it, he lost all radio contact with the Earth and with his crewmates. For about 48 minutes on every orbit he was cut off from every other person in the universe.

Collins later wrote that he did not feel lonely but aware, and even happy, in the quiet. He also knew his task if something went wrong on the surface: he would have to fly home alone. He was ready to try to rescue the lander if it reached orbit but could not reach him.

=== The way home | 21 to 24 July 1969 | A broken switch, a felt-tip pen and a splashdown in the Pacific.

Inside Eagle, the astronauts found that a small circuit breaker switch had been broken off, probably by a backpack bumping into it. It was the switch that armed the engine for lift-off. Aldrin pushed a felt-tip pen into the hole, and it worked.

On 21 July the top half of Eagle lifted off, using the bottom half, which stayed behind, as its launch pad. Its engine pushed hot gas downwards, and the gas pushed the craft up: the third law at work. Three and a half hours later it met Columbia in orbit, the two men climbed across with their boxes of rocks, and Eagle was set loose.

On 24 July Columbia hit the Earth's atmosphere at about 11 kilometres per second, glowing inside its heat shield. Under three parachutes it landed in the Pacific Ocean, where the aircraft carrier USS Hornet picked up the crew. Because nobody could be sure the Moon carried no germs, they spent the next three weeks in quarantine.

=== What it changed | 1969 to 1972 | Six landings, twelve people on the Moon, and nobody since.

Apollo 11 met Kennedy's goal with five months to spare. Five more crews landed between 1969 and 1972, and in all twelve people walked on the Moon. The rocks they brought back, more than 380 kilograms in total, are still being studied. They showed that the Moon is about as old as the Earth, and helped prove that it formed from debris after a huge collision with the young Earth.

After Apollo 17 in December 1972, nobody went back. The Space Race was won, the cost was high, and attention turned to space stations and robots. More than fifty years later, new missions are being prepared to return people to the Moon.
