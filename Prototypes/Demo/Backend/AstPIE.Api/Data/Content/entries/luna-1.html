=== The race to the Moon begins | 1958 | After the first satellites, the next goal was obvious: hit the Moon.

Once Sputnik had reached orbit, Sergei Korolev's design bureau set itself a far harder target: to send a probe all the way to the Moon, 384,000 kilometres away. The plan had a very simple first goal. The probe would not land or orbit. It would hit the Moon, and in doing so prove that Soviet rockets could reach another world.

The probe was a small metal sphere, like a larger Sputnik, called Ye-1. To get there it needed a more powerful rocket: the R-7 with a new upper stage on top, the Block E. That stage had to fire at exactly the right moment and stop at exactly the right speed, because a tiny error near the Earth becomes thousands of kilometres by the time the probe reaches the Moon.

It did not go well at first. In 1958 three launches were tried, in September, October and December. All three rockets failed before the probe could leave the Earth. The fourth attempt was ready at the start of the new year.

=== Escape speed | 2 January 1959 | For the first time, something made by people left the Earth behind.

On 2 January 1959 the rocket lifted off from Tyuratam, in Kazakhstan. This time every stage worked. The Block E pushed the probe up to about 11 kilometres per second, the speed needed to escape the Earth's gravity completely.

That speed is a turning point. Anything slower, however fast, will eventually fall back or stay in orbit. Anything faster will leave and never return. The probe, later called Luna 1, became the first object made by humans to reach it.

Go deeper: where does escape speed come from?

Gravity pulls a rising object back and slows it down. The higher it goes, the weaker that pull becomes. Escape speed is the speed at which the object slows down forever but never quite stops, so it never comes back.

It depends only on the mass and size of the planet, not on the object. From the Earth it is about 11.2 km/s, from the Moon only 2.4 km/s, and from Jupiter about 60 km/s.

=== An artificial comet | 3 January 1959 | To show the world where it was, the probe made a glowing cloud in space.

How do you prove that a small probe is on its way to the Moon? On 3 January, about 113,000 kilometres from Earth, Luna 1 released a kilogram of sodium vapour. In sunlight the cloud glowed orange, like the light of a street lamp, and spread out into a fuzzy patch hundreds of kilometres wide.

For a few minutes it was visible from the ground as a faint star in the constellation Aquarius, and astronomers photographed it. It was the first artificial comet, and it did two jobs at once: it let scientists check the probe's path, and it showed everyone that something really was on its way to the Moon.

=== The miss | 4 January 1959 | A timing mistake on the ground sent the probe 6,000 kilometres wide.

The upper stage was supposed to be switched off by a radio command from the ground at exactly the right moment. The command came a little late. The stage burned a few seconds too long, and the probe went slightly too fast.

On 4 January 1959, about 34 hours after launch, Luna 1 swept past the Moon at a distance of about 6,000 kilometres. Then it kept going. Too fast to be captured by the Moon, and fast enough to escape the Earth, it went into orbit around the Sun, between the orbits of the Earth and Mars, where it still travels today.

The Soviet press presented it as a triumph: the first spacecraft to escape the Earth, and the first artificial planet. It was given the name Mechta, "dream". Only years later did it become widely known that the real goal had been to hit the Moon.

=== What it found | January 1959 | Two discoveries: a wind from the Sun, and a Moon with no magnetic field.

Luna 1 carried a magnetometer, Geiger counters and small devices called ion traps that could catch charged particles. Its batteries lasted about 62 hours, long enough to send back measurements all the way out past the Moon.

The ion traps found a steady stream of charged gas flowing outward through space. It was the first direct measurement of the solar wind, a flow of particles from the Sun that fills the whole Solar System. Its existence had been predicted, and Luna 1 confirmed it.

Near the Moon, the magnetometer found no sign of a magnetic field. Unlike the Earth, the Moon has no global magnetic field, a result later probes confirmed many times over.

=== A second chance | September and October 1959 | Eight months later, Luna 2 did what Luna 1 had missed.

The next probe of the same design, Luna 2, was launched on 12 September 1959. This time the timing was right. On 14 September it crashed into the Moon, the first object from Earth to touch another world. It carried metal pennants with the Soviet coat of arms, scattered across the surface on impact.

Three weeks later Luna 3 flew around the Moon and took the first photographs of its far side, which is always turned away from the Earth. In a single year, the Luna programme had escaped the Earth, hit the Moon and seen its hidden face.
